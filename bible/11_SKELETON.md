# The skeleton

*Written 14 October 2026. Hutch has been studying favourite RPGs and essays on game design and ludonarrative, and asked for the skeletal structure of the ideal version of this game: the satisfying parts of an RPG, found by examining the meta, then polished and tightened so they don't overcomplicate what already works. Hutch's list is quoted in full at the end. Everything here is [PROPOSED] until Hutch says otherwise, and where it would change something DECIDED, it says so. Nothing in the build has changed for it.*

## The short of it

- **Every system is the job.** Each of the seventeen ideas becomes part of something a doorman already does, and together they fold into eight systems, every one grown from what Wednesday already plays. Nothing goes in that the job doesn't explain.
- **The rats are the spine.** Day 1's choice about the rats is the game's first split and its biggest. It decides the building, what he can do, what he hears, what he owes, and whether he's warned about the end. Fed rats breed, so the alliance carries its own clock.
- **Seven stats, set by his job interview and moved by how he lives.** Hutch's six and Intuition, small numbers, shown plainly. The voices stay, as the people who taught him: a check is a stat, a teacher and the state he's in. Intuition is the dial between the plain reading of the game and the strange one.
- **His inventory is his pockets, and they fill with what he takes off people.** Confiscation is the loot. Everything he carries belongs to somebody.
- **The door draws from a town.** About four hundred people generated once a game, with families, jobs, addresses, habits and grudges, so every stranger is somebody, the commonest fake is a real sibling's licence, and the island stays small.
- **A fight is a few seconds, judged by who saw it.** Each second freezes as a print he can look round; he acts with what his hands are doing; the goal is the door, not a win. Afterwards the witnesses, the phones and the bar's camera decide what it was, and the camera's footage is the only time the player sees him from outside.
- **Drink on three clocks.** Tonight, the morning after, the month: the blackout that lets other people tell him what he did, tolerance, the shakes. And three ways to hold a glass: drink it, refuse it, or hold it all night like the old man.
- **He walks the Sov.** First person, on foot, through a building that goes down to the cannery's pilings and up to the roof, opened by keys, trust and the rats. Outside, six places on the highway, each one a person. No markers, and nothing respawns.
- **The end: the Big One, and the job turned inside out** (Claude's pick; Hutch chooses). All month the ground and the town give it away. On the last Wednesday the ground moves during his shift, and the doorman who spent four weeks keeping people out has to get them out. Table 28 won't leave their table.

## The meta, and the one rule

The essays on ludonarrative keep coming back to one idea: a mechanic says something, and when it says the opposite of the story, the player feels the lie. (Clint Hocking named it ludonarrative dissonance in 2007, writing about BioShock.) So the rule for every system here is that it's the job: what it asks of the player is what the door asks of him. That's also what keeps the list tight. A feature that isn't the job doesn't go in.

What players come to RPGs for, and where each one lives in this game:

| What players come for | Where it is here |
|---|---|
| Getting stronger | Stats that move rarely and for reasons; voices let in; clothes and gear; standing that opens doors |
| A build of their own | What he claims at the interview, who sits in the five seats in his head, what he wears, how he drinks |
| Loot | What he takes off people at the door |
| Choices that change the world | Strands, hinges and crossings, the rats first |
| Exploring | One building, opened up from the pilings to the roof |
| Mastery | Reading the door and the room, which the player gets better at, not just him; the fights; the games |
| Spending | Six places on the strip, clothes with a read and a history, and money turning over at the end |
| Companions | The bartender, his partner and witness; table 28; the old man |
| A payoff | An end that reads the whole month |

### Seventeen ideas, eight systems

| System | What it already is | What it takes in |
|---|---|---|
| The rats | the bait and traps, Walter, the knot, the parley | choices that matter, the worldlines, the end's warning |
| His body | drinks, hurt, the print as his body | the stats, drink, what clothes do, his deaths |
| His head | the voices, the five seats, tension | the eccentrics' ideas, Intuition |
| The door | the stool, the licences, the binder | a busier door, people and licences generated |
| The room | the posts, things in his hands, the dice | objects with life, physics, exploring |
| His pockets | the props, cash, the lost-and-found box | the inventory, money, stores, clothes |
| The word | codes, witnesses, rumour, the incident log | the world reacting, the law after a fight, their deaths |
| The month | the calendar, the teller, the event | the end, the hard questions, the eccentrics' stories |

The fight and the games in the bar aren't systems of their own. A fight is the door, the room, his body and the word at once; a game in the bar is the room and his body, played as a conversation.

### Against bloat

1. **One building, deep, not wide.** The Sov from the pilings to the roof, and six places on the highway.
2. **Everything has a reason and an owner.** No junk, no loot tables, no random drops. Every object in the Sov and every thing he can carry is written.
3. **Everyone is somebody.** No generic patrons: the town is generated once, and everyone in it has a life whether he meets them or not.
4. **Nothing respawns and nothing repeats.** A broken glass stays broken until somebody sweeps it, the dead stay dead, and no line is said twice.
5. **Time is the price.** The clock moves when he moves. Every night he chooses what to miss.
6. **No markers, no list of chores, no map but his.** His notes, the floor sketch, and a napkin with the town drawn on it.
7. **No grinding.** Stats move rarely and for reasons, never because he did a thing fifty times.
8. **None of the usual furniture:** no experience points, no creation screen, no grid inventory, no crafting, no skill trees, no fast travel, no collectibles, no repeatable jobs.

Where Claude would push back on the list:

- **Worldlines shouldn't be shown as a tree or a flowchart.** The world shows them: who's in the room, what's broken, what people say.
- **His deaths shouldn't be gotchas.** Sierra killed players for stepping on the wrong pixel. Here a death is a risk the player saw and took.
- **Physics belongs where his hands meet things.** No ragdolls: a fight is serious.
- **The town outside stays tiny.** Six places is enough to need, and few enough to know.

## The month

Thirty days, Wednesday to Thursday, the first real shift on Friday (DECIDED). The props' placeholder dates put day 1 on Wednesday 4 November 2026, so the owner's "INSPECTOR ON THE 14TH" is Saturday, day 11. The teller years later (PROPOSED; open question 9) is kept as the voice that foreshadows.

- **His shifts:** four nights a week, Wednesday to Saturday, after the test shift and the first Friday and Saturday. Extra shifts are his call: the early shift with table 28, or a Sunday or Monday night for the owner. They pay in money and standing and cost him the day (DECIDED: the early shifts are his choice, at a cost).
- **His days:** the mornings and afternoons, and his nights off, are the rest of his life: the town, the stores, the range, his room, the people who aren't at the bar, and sleep. Daylight is short, and every errand costs part of it.
- **The loop:** by day he spends time and money getting ready; by night the job tests what he's got (stats, voices, gear, standing); at close the reckoning (built: the till's tape) says what it cost; and on Thursdays the paper says what the town made of it.
- **Nights have weights.** A Wednesday is slow, a Friday or Saturday is the whole bar, and a Sunday is the bar without its anchor. The set pieces: the test shift (day 1), the first real Friday (day 3), the inspector (day 11), the mill's last shift and its wake (day 24, the public date), the night the rats leave, if they leave (day 28), the last Wednesday (day 29), and the morning after (day 30).
- **The acts:** week one is the job, the town and the rats' first choice, and his stats get set. Weeks two and three thicken the strands: the man who recognises him in week two, the rats doubling or bargaining, the first fight that brings the law, the eccentrics, the tells. Week four is the public date, and then the end.

## Choices that matter: worldlines

Four scales of consequence, each built on the one under it:

1. **Lines.** Every choice changes something somebody says later (the minimum rule; `tools/audit.mjs` has held Wednesday to it since 4 October).
2. **Strands.** A person's story across the month: the kid and his family, Dennis and his wife, Cardigan and Calgary, the bartender, Good Shoes, each eccentric, the small stories at the door. A strand remembers where it left off and picks up on the next night it can.
3. **Hinges.** A few choices that split the world for the rest of the game. The rats are the first and the biggest. The others: whom the door serves (it becomes table 28's bar, the kids', or the residents'), how he drinks, whether the law has a file on him, and what he's claimed about his past.
4. **Crossings.** Scenes that exist only where two strands meet in particular states. If he has terms with the rats and barred the kid, the kid's lost phone turns up on the third step. If the constable has a file on him and the rats owe him, her notebook goes missing. This is how the worldlines complexify: not more branches, but scenes the combination makes.

The engine is the one already planned in `02_SYSTEMS.md`: storylets offered when their conditions hold, and rule-matched lines. A crossing is a storylet with conditions on more than one strand.

**How the world shows it:** the room (who's there, what's broken, what's propped), the word on him (built), his voices, and **Thursday's paper**. The island's weekly comes out on Thursdays, days 2, 9, 16 and 23 (on day 30 there isn't one): the police blotter, the obituaries, the classifieds, letters from the eccentrics, the tide table, the mill's notice. What he did turns up in it a week late and slightly wrong. (Papers, Please has its daily paper; this is the small-town weekly, a thing he can hold.)

### The rats are the spine

DECIDED: ignored, they double. He can parley with the Rat King at points through the game until the colony gets too huge, or exterminate them, which costs in other ways. The Rat King is both the knot in the wall behind table 28 and a man who seems to speak for it.

Hutch's word was paramount. So the rats aren't a quest. They're the axis the other lines turn on. Day 1 sets him on one of three courses, and he can change course once, at a price.

**Terms.** He parleys, and keeps them: food on the third step every night he works (it costs him, in money or the cook's fries), the kitchen door on the latch after the cook goes home (the cook's goodwill, the inspector's risk), no poison.

- He gets quiet: no rat seen on the inspector's day. He gets what they hear, which is everything said where there's a wall or a floor, and in a bar that's worth more than anything (the old man at the end of the bar already says they hear every word table 28 says). He gets favours: they chew, fetch, frighten, find and carry (the wires in a drunk's truck, the keys from a pocket, a rat on the bar at close to empty the room, coins from inside the walls, a note under a door). He gets the building's other ways (see Walking the Sov). And he gets the warning (see The end).
- It costs him this: fed rats breed, so terms grow the colony faster than neglect does, unless he bargains for their numbers, which costs him something else. The old man's asks grow with the colony, from the step and the latch to things that cut against the job: let this one in, keep that one out, look the other way, keep the inspector away from the wall behind table 28. Using what they hear makes him what the kids' code forbids: a rat. And when the colony gets too big, the terms end (DECIDED), and the rats are his problem again.

**War.** Bait, traps, the cook's pellet gun, the exterminator from up island.

- He gets the owner's money code and the cook's, the inspector's pass, and control.
- It costs him Walter, and table 28 with him; the dead in the walls (the smell on Friday, and the customers who leave over it); the old man; everything they would have heard; and the warning. Scratch reads killing the king as the worst sign there is, and whether the bad nights after it are luck or coincidence is left to the player.

**Neglect.** He does nothing, and they double (DECIDED): thirty on Wednesday, doubling every week or so. Droppings, then the sound in every wall, a rat on the bar in front of a customer, a rat in the single washroom, the kids' video of it that gets the Sov known on the mainland, the inspector, the owner. Past a point the old man stops coming down, because there's nothing left to talk terms about.

**Changing course.** Breaking terms is the worst course of all: they hear everything, so they know. War can turn into terms once, if the old man will still come down, and it costs something he'd rather not give.

**The knot.** The war's last act is opening the wall behind table 28's chairs, with the table watching. It ends the rats for good and costs the most: the table's oldest secret, whatever the old man is, and the one thing in the building that knew what was coming.

**Why it's paramount:** the rats touch everything else. Table 28 feeds them without knowing it. The kitchen door is the cook's. The money is the owner's. The inspector is the law, for rats. The kids' code is about rats. The residents share their walls. The bartender has always known. And the old man talks about the last time the ground moved as if he'd been there.

## Him: seven stats

Hutch's six and Intuition, which is Hutch's nod to Disco's Inland Empire. This replaces "no skill levels" (PROPOSED in `01_PILLARS.md` and `02_SYSTEMS.md`): the stats are his body and his mind, and the voices stay, as what he's learned and from whom.

| Stat | What it is | At the Sov |
|---|---|---|
| Strength | Force | Holding a man, moving one who won't go, a full keg up the stairs |
| Constitution | What he can take | Drink, a punch, the cold on the door at two, the fourth night running |
| Dexterity | His hands | The glass caught before it lands, a wrist taken clean, darts, the range |
| Intelligence | Working it out | A licence's dates, the count, money, the arithmetic in a lie, the incident log |
| Wisdom | Judgment | What someone means to do, and what he means to do himself; when to step in |
| Charisma | Presence | Rapport in the twenty seconds at the door, talking a man down, a no that lands soft |
| Intuition | His gut | The room turning before it turns, the building, the rats, what's coming |

Intelligence and Intuition start with the same letters, so neither is shortened on screen.

**A check is a stat, a teacher and his state.** Two dice, plus the stat, plus whichever seated voice lends what it taught him (THE FIGHTER to Strength and Dexterity, THE MANUAL to Intelligence, THE FATHER to Wisdom and to Charisma at the door, THE WRONG MAN to Intuition, THE TABLE to Charisma and Constitution with drink in him), plus what he's wearing, less drink and hurt where they count. The tension in his head still widens the swing both ways, and stays felt in the print rather than shown as a number. Every check's tag says where its number comes from. The targets rise to fit.

**The scale.** Each stat runs from 1 to 5. An ordinary man is a 2. He starts at about 2 with a few 3s, and getting anything to 5 costs him something.

**How he starts: the interview.** No screen of points. On the test shift the bartender, the owner on the phone and table 28 ask what anyone asks a new doorman: ever been in a fight, where did you work, why did you leave, do you drink, what would you do if. His answers set his stats, the way his claims already become facts people hold him to (`02_SYSTEMS.md`, the past). Claim the city door and the fights, and he starts strong and people expect it of him; lose a fight, and they say so. It's Harry Du Bois' template done through the job (DECIDED: semi-tabula rasa, recalling and inventing who he was and is).

**How they move.** Rarely, maybe eight times in a game, each time with a line in the text, and both ways:

- Strength: up with the work (kegs, stairs, walking men out), down with a broken hand.
- Constitution: up with drinking, which is the trap (see Drink), and down with the month's damage.
- Dexterity: up at the range and the dartboard, down with the shakes.
- Intelligence: up with what he reads (the texts that are also voices), down with the blackouts.
- Wisdom: up when he owns a mistake out loud (`owned_it`, already counted), down with the lies he tells himself (the hidden `cocoon`).
- Charisma: up as the groups warm to him, down when the word on him turns.
- Intuition: up each time he listens to what can't be explained (the old man, the knot, the nights at the door), down each time he explains it away.

**Passive reads.** Past a threshold, a voice speaks unasked. At Wisdom 3 he notices the friend at the rail has stopped drinking. At Intuition 4 he hears the glasses ring on the back bar at three in the morning, when there are no trucks. The build decides what the player is told.

**Intuition is the dial.** Low, and the old man is an old drunk who talks about rats, the knot is rats knotted by their tails, and the end comes out of nowhere. High, and the knot talks, the building has a voice, and he sees the end coming for weeks. Both are honest playthroughs, and the player sets the dial by what he chooses to believe. The game never says which is true (PROPOSED since 26 September: whether the knot talks is never confirmed).

**Where the player sees them:** a page in his notes, in his hand, with the old number struck through each time one moves, so the page keeps his history.

## What he carries

The inventory is his pockets.

- **The pockets:** a jacket with two outside pockets and an inside one, and his jeans. Each holds one thing or a few small ones. What's in his right outside pocket is in his hand fastest (the flashlight). What's in the inside pocket nobody sees, until somebody pats him down. Handling works the way the licences already do: take it out, turn it over, look closer, use it on what he's looking at.
- **His room upstairs** holds the rest: the bag he came with, the dresser, under the mattress. The night clerk has a key to every room.
- **The lost-and-found box** behind the bar: everything left behind is a story, a lead or something to sell (`02_SYSTEMS.md`).
- **Where things come from:** what he takes off people (most of it), what he buys, what he's given, what he finds, and what the rats bring. A doorman's loot is confiscation: the fake licence, the knife at the door, the bag from the single washroom, the drunk's keys, the mickey brought in, a vape, a phone. All of it belongs to somebody, and somebody may come back for it.
- **Four things about every item:** whose it is and how he came by it; its heat, the trouble it is if it's found on him (a seized bag, someone else's keys, a fifty folded into a licence); what it does, to a person, a thing or a check; and what it's worth, to the pawn shop, to its owner, and after the end, which changes what everything's worth.

A few of them: the flashlight (the hologram under its beam, the eyes in the keg room, and a weapon in law if he swings it); a pen (the incident log, a number on a coaster); a lighter (a smoke shared on the deck is ten minutes of rapport); the big man's truck keys (his daughter waiting in the lot, and the big man at two in the morning, asking for them back); a rat's tooth on a string from the old man (Intuition, and table 28 can smell rat on him).

## Things in the room

Fewer things, each one deep. Every object in the Sov gets:

1. **A reason it's there:** who put it there and when. The coaster in the deck door is the smokers'. The brick on the bin lid is the old man's. The tape by the door is the last doorman's arithmetic.
2. **States that stay:** propped, latched, locked, broken, fixed, wet, moved. The room remembers.
3. **Two uses or more,** one of them not the obvious one. The fire extinguisher puts out a fire, holds a door, ends a fight, and is assault with a weapon if he uses it on a man.
4. **A read from each voice:** THE FIGHTER sees a weapon, THE MANUAL a liability, THE FATHER what it says about the owner, THE WRONG MAN the story it's in.
5. **Consequences:** use it, break it or leave it, and somebody notices.

There are about forty objects in the Sov. The ones already built come up to this standard first: the stool and the ledge, the tape, the scanner that keeps every card it's shown, the three propped doors, the jukebox, the pool table and its lean, the dartboard with its chalked doors, the dead payphone, the binder, the till, the office and its mirror, the third step. Then the camera over the bar and its broken recorder; the first-aid kit with the naloxone in it; the bell and the lights at close; the back-bar mirror; and the bar clock, set fast the way bar clocks are.

**Every problem has three ways through or more,** by talk, force, things, people or the rats, and each way leaves something different behind.

- The man who won't leave at close: talk him out (Charisma); walk him out (Strength); bring the lights up and kill the music; hand him his coat; ring his wife from the payphone, if he's had it fixed; ask table 28 to take him home; or, with terms, a rat on the bar.
- The man who means to drive: take his keys (Dexterity, or a hand on his shoulder and Charisma); tell his daughter in the truck; ring the one cab in town; push the dumpster across his truck (Strength); let the air out of a tire; buy him one more and let table 28 keep him till he sleeps; have the rats eat through a wire; or let him go, and read Thursday's paper.

**Physics where the body meets things.** The dice already fall as solid cubes, worked out before anyone sees them so the same throw lands the same way every time (`godot/game/dice_sim.gd`). The same goes for what he handles and what gets knocked about: a glass caught or dropped, coins, keys thrown, the balls on the felt, darts, a bottle off the back bar, a table going over in a fight. People are never ragdolls; bodies in a fight are holds and positions. Physics has its big moment once, at the end, when the back bar comes down.

## The door, busier: the town

**The town is generated once a game.** About four hundred adults: names, ages, faces from parts, families (brothers, sisters, exes, kids), work (the mill, the ferry, the hospital, the Esso, none), addresses on the town's streets, how they drink, a debt, a grudge, one secret. Everyone the door sees is one of them. Over thirty nights the regulars show themselves by coming back, some never come twice, and some die and are in Thursday's paper.

**Three kinds of people:**

- **The cast,** about twenty, written across the month: table 28, the staff, the kids, the big man, the old man, the eccentrics.
- **The returning:** a deck of about forty small stories, of which a game meets perhaps fifteen, each two to four visits long and pinned to somebody from the town. The man in a wedding suit, alone, three Fridays running. The girl who turns nineteen at midnight and is at the door at a quarter to. The ferry worker stuck on the island since his sailing was cancelled. The woman who watches the door every Saturday for her ex. A small story starts at the door and goes on only if he takes it up.
- **The passing:** generated for one night, with one reason they came, one true detail he can read (sawdust on the boots, a hospital bracelet, a ring's pale line, a ferry stub, paint on the hands), and one tie to somebody else in town. If he takes an interest, a passer can be dealt a small story and come back.

**Why they won't feel like NPCs:** each has a reason, a detail and a tie; each remembers how he treated them and tells somebody; each goes on living whether he sees them or not; and none of them says the same line twice (they talk by age, work and place, under the rules in `10_VOICES.md`).

**The waves.** A Friday might put a hundred people through the door, but they come in waves with reasons: the hockey ending, the mill's swing shift letting out, the Legion closing at midnight and sending its drinkers up the highway, the last ferry. In a wave there's a line in the rain behind the glass. Most people are a quick read, and one or two in each wave are a story. He decides whom to read slowly, and the line builds while he does (PROPOSED since 27 September: slow checking builds a line in the rain, fast checking lets mistakes through).

**Licences from the town.** Every licence belongs to someone in it: their face, younger; their street; their birthday. The fakes are the real kinds, each with a tell a careful player can find. The older sibling's real licence is the commonest fake there is, and the town knows the sibling ("You're not Kayla Moffat. Kayla drove you here last Wednesday."). Then: a date lifted under the laminate; a hologram that's only ink and sits still when the card tips; a licence from out of province, which THE MANUAL knows and the player has to take on trust; an expired one; a photo on a phone. The regulars he knows by face never show one.

**The province tests the door.** In B.C. the Liquor and Cannabis Regulation Branch sends minors in to try to buy, with inspectors watching, and a licensee who serves them is penalised ([the Minors as Agents Program](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/employment-business/business/liquor-regulation-licensing/inspections-and-enforcement/minors-as-agents-program)). Any nineteen-year-old at the door could be one. The penalty lands on the owner, and the owner's temper lands on him.

(The hologram Hutch says doesn't show is a bug in the build, and The game's future form is fixing it.)

## Hands on: the fight

What it has to be true to, until Hutch says otherwise: a door fight is short, ugly and mostly settled before it starts; the job is getting a man out the door with the least force, not winning; the danger is usually the friend nobody watched; and what happens afterwards depends on who saw it. Hutch's own account is asked for below and comes first. This is a frame to hang it on.

**1. The read.** Before anyone touches anyone. The room's heat is already simulated (the pool table runs from calm to violence over a night). In first person he looks, and the voices mark what he looks at: THE FIGHTER's slash on a hand that's closing, THE FATHER's ring round the friend at the rail, THE MANUAL's label on the way out. Most fights end here, with a word, a stance, standing close, or the lights.

**2. Hands on.** The moment he touches someone, or someone swings. From here the law is watching.

**3. The seconds.** The fight runs a second at a time. Each second the press stops and prints the frame: the room mid-motion, frozen. For a breath of real time he can look round it, and he only knows what he looks at (the glass in the friend's hand, the phones coming up, the door). Then he does one thing with what his hands are doing. Hands free: step off the line, cover, take a wrist, get behind him. Holding a wrist: turn him, walk him, put him on the wall, let go. From behind: walk him, or take him down, which is a risk. At any point: say something, shout for the bartender, or hit him, which is the last thing. Each is a check (a stat, the voice that taught it, his state). The other man's next move comes from his own state and his own roll, and the next frame prints. If the breath runs out before he's chosen, he freezes, which is a thing that happens to people. Most fights are three to six frames, and they end out the door, apart, on the floor, or with him on it.

**4. The witnesses.** Who saw, from where, and what they'll say. Everyone in the room has a line of sight; the kids have their phones up; and there's the camera over the bar, if its recorder works. (The owner's broken box under the till is an object with consequences: fixed, its footage can clear him or hang him.) Each frame he acted in is weighed against what was coming at him. Most of the time nothing happens, as Hutch says. Sometimes there's a complaint, a video posted, a hospital, the constable, and then the footage, the witnesses' versions and his incident log are laid side by side, and the log is the one he wrote. It runs from nothing, to a word from the owner, to a file at the detachment, to a charge, to his security licence suspended, which means no door and no pay.

**The one time you see him.** He's seen only in reflections (PROPOSED with the form, 27 September). When the footage is pulled, the player watches the fight again from the camera over the bar, printed in the same three inks, high and grey and silent: the only time in the game he's seen from outside, doing what he did. Set beside the log he wrote, it's the gap the hidden measure has been keeping all along.

**The walk.** To try, and to cut if it isn't good: once he has a good hold, the frames stop and the player steers the man to the door in real time, through the room, the man pulling, the tables in the way, the door to get open. It's the commonest physical thing in the job.

**Why it isn't borrowed:** there's no health bar to empty and no win to score. The weapon is attention, the goal is a door, and the judge is everyone who saw.

## Drink

DECIDED: he drinks, and must immerse himself in the culture and the excess to understand what he's doing. His father was an alcoholic who became a dry alcoholic.

Drink keeps what it means in the story, belonging and its cost, and gets three clocks:

- **Tonight.** Each drink goes in and comes out with time and food. One or two loosen him: Charisma and Intuition up, THE TABLE warmer. Three or four: Dexterity, Wisdom and Intelligence down, and the print slips (built). Five or six: everything physical down, his Charisma turned against him, the page swaying (built). Past that, blackouts: a choice can skip time, he comes to somewhere else, and other people tell him what he did. Not all of them tell the truth, and what he said while he was out counts as said.
- **The morning after.** The hangover is the next day's daylight: the paper too bright, the ink too heavy, Constitution and Intelligence down till noon.
- **The month.** Tolerance: drink most nights and it takes more to feel it and less of it shows, while the damage goes on underneath. Dependence: after enough of it, a dry night brings the shakes (Dexterity down, his head louder). Stopping cold after a month of rye can kill him (see Deaths).

**Three ways to hold a glass:** drink it; refuse it, and the culture closes (table 28 don't trust a man who won't drink) while his head stays clear; or hold it all night without drinking, like the old man at the end of the bar. The Ink already notices that one: "You held a glass at that table and did not drink it. Like me."

**The story:** whom he drinks with is whose voice he earns. The bartender drinks too, and knows exactly what his drinking looks like. THE FATHER, who stopped and kept everything else, is against it, and isn't always right. What the teller is drinking, years later, is the game's last picture.

## Money, the strip and what he wears

DECIDED: money and skill-building in novel ways. PROPOSED in `02_SYSTEMS.md`: the currency fails, and the last thing that holds value is people.

- **In:** wages, paid on Fridays with the room's rent taken off first, which is the owner's hold on him; tips (table 28 tip to own you); the door bribe he takes or doesn't; bets (pool, darts, the range, arm wrestling); what he sells (the pawn shop takes the lost-and-found, and what he confiscated, if he's that kind of doorman); and what the rats find in the walls.
- **Out:** rent; his tab; food; the rats' food on the third step; clothes; gear; rounds and gifts (a round for table 28 is a statement); bets; fines; what he pays to fix in the Sov (the camera's recorder, the deck door's latch, the payphone); and a debt from before, which the man who recognises him in week two knows about.

**The strip.** Six places on the highway, in walking distance, each one a person, each a daylight visit:

1. The Sov's own cold beer and wine store, through the door behind the bar.
2. The Esso across the highway: coffee, smokes, scratch tickets, batteries, and one of the kids on nights.
3. The hospital auxiliary's thrift store, for clothes. Its racks are the town's dead and divorced. When a man from town dies, his coat turns up there within the week, and the woman at the till knows whose it was.
4. The pawn shop: buys and sells, no questions, mostly.
5. The hardware store: padlocks, a better flashlight, gloves, traps and bait (the war is bought here), duct tape, a door closer.
6. The diner: breakfast after a shift, and where the town talks in the morning.

The rod and gun club is out the highway, too far to walk. Places that far need somebody to take him, so the map past the strip is a question of who'll give him a ride.

**What he wears.** Clothes are the main thing money buys, and every piece does two things: a stat (one up or down, or a bonus in one place: the door, the floor, a fight) and a read (how each group takes it).

- The owner's black SECURITY shirt: authority at the door; to the kids, a narc; and the drunk who wants a go at security picks him.
- A work flannel: the island's own uniform. Rapport with the locals, no authority.
- A good wool coat from the thrift store: table 28 take him seriously. It was a dead man's, and his widow sees it.
- Steel toes: steadier on his feet, slower, and a kick in steel toes is a different charge.
- Slash-resistant gloves: safer patting someone down and in the single washroom. A man in gloves looks like he expects a fight, and a constable reads them as intent.
- A good raincoat: Constitution on the door in the rain, which is every night.
- Reading glasses: he's at the age where the birthdays on licences are small. Intelligence at the door, and table 28 never let it go.
- A ring: Strength in a fight, and the cut it leaves is evidence.
- A watch: the time without a phone, which matters after the end, and something to be robbed of.

Clothes wear, get wet, tear, and take blood, which prints in the red ink and stays till he washes it out or buys another.

**After the end, money turns over.** Cash is worth less every day. Batteries, a flashlight, a lighter, a watch, beer and a good coat are worth more. The people he's stood by are worth most. The player who spent on people comes out ahead of the one who saved.

## Walking the Sov

DECIDED (27 September): through his eyes, from his posts, not a free walk. Hutch now asks for freer exploration, designed around a first-person RPG view. This proposes changing that part of the DECIDED form and keeping the rest.

- **He walks,** first person and on foot, through the whole building. The numbered ways round go. The posts stay as the places the job is done from (the stool, the end of the bar, the rail by the pool table): he sits or leans there, and the room comes to him.
- **The clock moves when he moves.** Walking and watching move it; talk moves it line by line; his notes stop it.
- **A doorman doesn't run in a bar.** He can, and the room sees it: running means something's wrong, heads turn, and the word on him says he ran.
- **The building goes down and up.** The bar, the hall, the kitchen, the deck, the lot and the front desk. Below: the keg room, the office, the old foundation and, past where the concrete gives out to dirt, the pilings of the cannery that stood here first, where the tide went in and out under the floor (`table28.ink`). Above: the stairs, two floors of rooms and the people in them, his own room, the roof.
- **What opens it:** keys (Terry's board); trust (a resident who asks him in); the rats (with terms, the old man shows him the building's other ways: the crawlspace under the floor, the dumbwaiter shaft from the old dining room, the walk under the pilings to the alley, the gap behind the back bar where a room can be heard through the wall); and force (a door he breaks stays broken, and somebody pays for it).
- **The town** is the strip, by day, on foot. His map is a napkin the bartender draws him on day 2 ("Esso. Thrift. Hardware. Don't go to the Legion."), and he adds to it.
- **No markers, and nothing respawns.** The depth is in time and state: the same room at four in the afternoon and at two in the morning, before a fight and after it, before the end and after it.

One of Hutch's favourites, Symphony of the Night, is one castle opened up deeper and deeper and then turned upside down. The Sov is that, small: one building opened across a month, and after the end, the same rooms in a new state.

## Deaths

Two kinds, and they work differently.

**His.** Rare, and earned: a choice the game marks as one that can kill him, and a failed roll, with his body's state in the odds. The ways men like him actually die, told the way the teller would tell them: down the keg room stairs with a full keg of lager, which is how two men in this town have died already; the knife in the lot he didn't see because he was watching the phone; stopping cold after a month of rye; the one punch, and the floor. When he dies, the night's last entry in the incident log is in the bartender's hand, about him. Then it's morning again: the night is lost, and played again from the start of that day. One save after every choice stays as it is; dying is the only way back, and it costs the whole night. Every death he's had is kept at the back of the log.

**Theirs.** The kid who overdoses in the single washroom on the night he skipped the checks. The man who drove. A one-punch from the other side. The man put out into the cold. Walter. These are never undone. They're in Thursday's paper, and they haunt (`02_SYSTEMS.md`: a presence that comes back at bad moments and, carried long enough, turns into something useful).

No death is random. Each comes from a choice and a roll the player saw.

## Games in the bar

Five, and each one is a place where people talk.

1. **Pool.** The big man's table: bets, the hustle, losing on purpose (the Ink counts it already: `bigman_ease`), and the fight it can start. The felt and cushions are already solid in the dice's sim.
2. **Darts.** The chalked scores on the board's doors. Quick, for money, with table 28 or the kids. Drunk, the board doubles in the print, and he has to throw at the right one.
3. **The range.** Hutch's target practice. Table 28 are never at the bar on Sundays (DECIDED); in this draft they're at the rod and gun club out the highway (it's November, and deer season), and he can go if he's asked. Paper targets, printed. Shooting well raises Dexterity, earns the table's respect and a voice, and brings a rifle within reach of the story. In a war on the rats, the same game is the cook's pellet gun in the keg room at three in the morning.
4. **Arm wrestling.** A challenge at the bar: Strength and Constitution, money, standing and the crowd. It can break an arm.
5. **Crib.** With Cardigan, the quiet one, who talks while he pegs. Slow, and he says the things he doesn't say at the table.

**Against tedium.** Each is short (minutes), rare (a handful of times a game), played for something that matters (money, standing, a voice, something learned), changed by his body (drink doubles the target), and after the first time can be left to his stats with one key. And each is a conversation, so how he plays colours what they say. Beat the big man too easily and he stops talking; lose on purpose and he talks more.

## The eccentrics

Six, and the old man. Each is a person before an idea (no caricatures: every one gets their best voice, and the game holds them dear, `01_PILLARS.md`). Each has an idea the player can argue with, each sees one face of what's coming, and each can leave a voice in his head, through their talk or their text (DECIDED: voices come from people, books and pamphlets). Their stories are strands of three to five meetings. None of them hands out quests.

1. **The Living Man** won't show a licence at the door: he's a living man, not a legal person, and he never consented to be identified. A freeman on the land, from a pamphlet and ten years of videos, after the bank took the house and the house took the marriage. The idea: authority is a story people agree to. He's the door's own philosopher, he's kind to the residents, and when the empire's paper stops meaning anything, he's the only one who was ready. Voice: THE PAMPHLET.
2. **The Lightkeeper,** on leave from a staffed light up the strait (B.C. still keeps lightkeepers), drinking between rotations. He watches weather and water for a living and keeps a log. The idea: attention as a discipline; the sea doesn't care whether you watch it, so you watch it properly. He sees the physical tells first: the tide, the birds, the colour of the water. Voice: THE LOG.
3. **The Gold Man** sells silver rounds out of his truck and pays for his rounds in silver. Money is a promise the capital makes and won't keep. He's right about the currency, he has a generator, and he's frightened for his daughter. Voice: THE NEWSLETTER.
4. **The Faller and the Treesitter.** A man who's fallen old growth for forty years and a young woman from the blockade, who argue at the bar every Tuesday and buy each other drinks. Both love the forest; one cut it to feed his kids, one sits in it to save it. The ideas: what a place is for, and time measured in cedars. The faller felt the ground move under a cut block in October and told nobody. Two voices, at odds with each other.
5. **The Preacher,** a lay preacher from the church on the highway, comes in on Fridays with tracts and buys the drunks coffee. He sat at table 28 once, in the sixth chair, before he stopped drinking: a dry alcoholic who found God where the father found nothing. The idea: apocalypse means an uncovering. He says the end is coming, and in this game he isn't wrong. Voice: THE TRACT.
6. **The Esso kid,** one of the kids, twenty, on nights at the Esso, reads physics on his phone between customers. He believes time is a block, that everything that will happen already has, and he'd call them worldlines. The idea: is anything he does a choice, if the story's already been told? That's the teller's frame, argued at four in the morning over a slushie. He's the one who notices the clocks. Voice: THE BLOCK.
7. **The old man at the end of the bar,** already written: rats outlast walls, and walls outlast empires.

## Hard questions

The job asks them every night, and the game's work is not to answer them. Every group judges by its own code, so every answer is honour to someone and betrayal to someone else (`02_SYSTEMS.md`).

- The kid in the rain would be safer inside than in the truck. Is the door for him or for the room?
- Dennis is drinking himself to death at table 28, and the table pays your wages. Do you cut him off?
- His wife is at the door. Do you tell her where he is?
- The dealer in the single washroom keeps the kids off worse. Do you call it in?
- The rats hear everything. Do you use it?
- Do you hit him first? (THE FIGHTER: the first one decides it.)
- Do you write it down the way it happened?
- Do you drink?
- On the last night, whom do you let in, and whom do you make leave?

The emotional stakes are particular people the player comes to love, put at risk by what the player does: Walter, the kid, Dennis, the bartender, the old man, the table. The themes come with the setting without anyone hunting for them: addiction, and the father's version of it; the opioid crisis in a small town (the single washroom, the naloxone in the first-aid kit); violence and the law; men losing work and a town losing its mill; the old and the young; sex and money in the rooms upstairs; grief. Debauchery as it really is, and no more (DECIDED).

## The end: what's coming

DECIDED: a narrator foreshadows an event that changes everything. OPEN until now: what it is (question 8 in `05_LOG.md`). Hutch, 14 October: "there is something happening the whole time through the playthrough that hints at the apocalypse or an epochal shift in day to day reality that will change everything about the mental, emotional and physical landscape of reality, time, society."

**What the event has to do:**

1. Happen in every playthrough, so the teller can promise it (PROPOSED: the event is fixed; what it does to everyone isn't).
2. Change the ground, time, the town and the people in it, as Hutch put it.
3. Show from day 1, through the world rather than through anybody explaining it.
4. Belong to this island and the present day, and not be cyberpunk (DECIDED).
5. Make the old saying true: rats leave a sinking ship (PROPOSED: if he's allied with them, they're gone the night before).
6. Make the door matter more than it ever has.
7. Leave table 28 at their table (DECIDED: it sustains itself to the end).

**Claude's pick: the Big One, and the quiet after it.** The island sits over a fault that breaks every few hundred years, and people say it's overdue; everyone on the coast knows it and lives as if it won't come. On the last Wednesday, day 29, four weeks to the night after his test shift, the ground moves during his shift, at the door. Three minutes of it. The lights go, the back bar comes down, and every car alarm in the lot goes off. Then the quiet: no power, no phones, no network, no card machine, no ferry, no word from the mainland. The empire doesn't fall. It stops reaching the island.

What it does:

- **The ground.** The coast drops. The Sov, built on the old cannery's pilings, shifts and leans. Hours later the sea comes up under the floor, higher than any king tide.
- **Time.** Before, and after. Once the phones die, the time is the bar clock, which runs on a battery and was always set fast; then the tide and the light. At close the till can't print without power, so the last reckoning is in his hand, by candle, on the back of the incident log.
- **The town.** Cash means what people agree it means. The Sov is the one lit building on the highway (candles, and the Gold Man's generator if the Gold Man is a friend). The door becomes the town's first border.
- **The people.** The young lose the world they lived in, which was in their phones, and the old get back the one they remember. Table 28 know how to live without a network, and the kids don't. The voices of the old, which couldn't read the young (`02_SYSTEMS.md`), are suddenly the ones that know what to do.

**The last night turns the job inside out.** For four weeks his work is keeping people out and knowing who's in. Once the ground has moved, the right thing is probably to get everyone out of an old wooden building on pilings and up the hill before the water comes. Nobody's sure, and that's the decision. Clearing the room is the closing skill he's practised every night (`02_SYSTEMS.md`), and now it saves lives. Who will go for him depends on the month: who trusts him, who owes him, whom he's 86'd, whom he knows by face, now there are no licences left to check. And table 28 won't leave their table. "We were here first." He can't make them, unless they'd follow him anywhere. Either way the table is there in the morning.

**The rats.** With terms, the old man tells him a week before that they'll be going, and on Tuesday night, day 28, the keg room is empty and the wall behind table 28 is quiet. If he listened, he knows. At war, there's no warning. Neglected, they all go at once on Wednesday afternoon, out every door in daylight, a river of rats across the highway, and the whole town sees it and laughs, or doesn't.

**Day 30, the morning after:** the town, the sea, who's alive. The reckoning reads the whole month (PROPOSED: the spike, with every night's tape on it), and the teller is revealed (PROPOSED: who's telling it depends on the playthrough).

**The public date.** Everyone counts down to something else (PROPOSED: the public date and the private event aren't the same thing). Claude's pick is the mill's last shift. The smokers on the deck say Christmas (`deck.ink`); the notice is in Thursday's paper on day 9: Friday 27 November, day 24. The wake that night is the biggest night of the month at the door, and everybody thinks it's the end of something. It ended on a Wednesday.

**The tells,** all month, on every layer:

- *The ground:* glasses ringing on the back bar when there are no trucks; the deck door that won't latch because the frame's moved; the pool table's lean getting worse; the water in the keg room a little higher every week; a swarm of tiny tremors under the island in week two, on the TV news, which the seismologists say is normal (it is: under the real island a swarm like it comes [about every fourteen months](https://gsa.confex.com/gsa/2007CD/webprogram/Paper121326.html)); king tides; birds off the strait; dogs.
- *The island:* the card machine (built, on day 1); the truck that didn't come off the ferry; the island running short (Hutch's notes, built: the diesel, limes in eighths, NO MORE TIL TUES over a tap); sailings cancelled; the mainland's news getting shorter.
- *Time:* the bar clock against the phones, and the Esso's clock against both.
- *The strange:* dreams the voices share at night; the old man's stories of the last time; the rats; and each eccentric seeing one face of it.
- *The teller:* "That was the first time the glasses rang. Nobody counted it."

**What he notices is up to him.** Intuition decides how many tells he sees and how much the voices make of them, and drink raises it. The Lightkeeper and the Faller teach him the ground, the Esso kid the clocks, the old man the rest. A doorman with low Intuition who never asks gets the end as a surprise. Both are honest.

**The other two candidates:**

- **The quiet alone.** No quake. One night the power, the network and the ferries go and don't come back, and nobody ever agrees why. Subtler and stranger, with everything above but the shock and the sea.
- **A modern myth.** After Jung's book on flying saucers: something seen over the strait that everyone describes differently, the sign of an age turning, never confirmed. The most metaphysical of the three, and the hardest to make land in a bar.

## The people's look

Hutch likes his hand, which looks like wood grain. Honestly, it's half accident. The press cuts people in strokes that wrap round their form, the way a lino cutter follows a figure (`godot/look/surface.gdshader`, the cut for people). His hands are the nearest thing to his eyes, so the strokes are at their biggest there, and they bend round the knuckles and the back of the hand like growth rings round a knot. Nobody planned for it to read as carved wood. It's the clearest the style has ever looked, though, and it's the way to cut everyone: people cut at the scale the strokes have on his hand, so faces and bodies read as carved wood, the whittled figures the look test was reaching for. That's for the build to try, in The game's future form, which owns the build. Until then the hand stays as it is.
