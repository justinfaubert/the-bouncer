# The skeleton

*Written 14 October 2026, and rewritten that evening with Hutch's answers. Hutch has been studying favourite RPGs and essays on game design and ludonarrative, and asked for the skeletal structure of the ideal version of this game: the satisfying parts of an RPG, found by examining the meta, then polished and tightened so they don't overcomplicate what already works. Hutch's list is quoted in full at the end.*

*Hutch's answer to the first draft: "this is fantastic. All of it." So the skeleton is the game's direction (DECIDED, 14 October). Where Hutch decided a particular, it's marked DECIDED here and in `05_LOG.md`. Everything else is Claude's draft and stays [PROPOSED] until Hutch says otherwise. Hutch's answers word for word, and the stories from Hutch's own doors and bars, are in `12_FROM_THE_DOOR.md`. The build fixes in them (the jukebox, the dice tray, the camera) went to The game's future form, which owns the build.*

## The short of it

- **Every system is the job.** Each of the seventeen ideas becomes part of something a doorman already does, and together they fold into eight systems, every one grown from what Wednesday already plays. Nothing goes in that the job doesn't explain.
- **The rats are the spine, and they mean something** (DECIDED). Day 1's choice about the rats is the game's first split and its biggest. They're also the game's metaphor, in Hutch's words "about humanity, ignorance, disconnection from nature, the cost of civilization", and the death of one. The game never says so.
- **It opens in the boss's office, through the boss's eyes** (DECIDED). Gary Van Dusen interviews him, away from the Sov. The answers set his seven stats, and it's hard not to get the job, but not impossible.
- **Seven stats, each 1 to 10, trained on purpose at a cost** (DECIDED). The classic six and Gut Brain, which is Hutch's name for Intuition. The voices stay, as the people who taught him.
- **His hand, his pockets and what tempts him.** He holds a thing and decides what to do with it and what to think about it (DECIDED: the handling matters). Confiscation is the loot, the lost-and-found box is his (DECIDED), and the job is full of things he shouldn't take.
- **A real little town** (DECIDED). Fixed streets, about four hundred people generated on them once a game, and not every small story in every game.
- **A fight is presence first, then seconds** (DECIDED: seconds, each one a choice). Most fights end with a big hand on a shoulder or a man standing up. When one goes physical, each second freezes and the choices are drawn on the frame: the circle (aikido), the blow (Krav Maga) or the choke, each with its own consequences. Then the witnesses, the camera, and a constable who comes to trust him or not (DECIDED).
- **Drink as it really is.** Nearly everyone drinks on shift, the prude is only mildly respected, and the owner pours the shots. Three clocks keep the cost.
- **He looks where he likes and goes where he looks** (DECIDED: "not a free walk, but not a limited walk either").
- **The end is the Big One, and maybe something helped it** (DECIDED). It's fixed in every game, and what it does to him and the town depends on what he chose, didn't choose and didn't notice. Something in the sky a week before makes its timing suspect. The doorman who kept people out all month has to get them out, and the young lose the world they lived in while the old get back the one they remember, though it's new to both.
- **The game knows who made it** (DECIDED: acknowledged). At the very end, once, and plainly.

## The meta, and the one rule

The essays on ludonarrative keep coming back to one idea: a mechanic says something, and when it says the opposite of the story, the player feels the lie. (Clint Hocking named it ludonarrative dissonance in 2007, writing about BioShock.) So the rule for every system here is that it's the job: what it asks of the player is what the door asks of him. That's also what keeps the list tight. A feature that isn't the job doesn't go in.

What players come to RPGs for, and where each one lives in this game:

| What players come for | Where it is here |
|---|---|
| Getting stronger | Stats that move rarely and for reasons, training he chooses and pays for, voices let in, clothes and gear, and standing that opens doors |
| A build of their own | What he says in Gary's office, who sits in the five seats in his head, what he wears, how he drinks, how he fights |
| Loot | What he takes off people at the door, and the lost-and-found box |
| Choices that change the world | Strands, hinges and crossings, the rats first; and what he doesn't choose or doesn't notice |
| Exploring | One building opened up from the pilings to the roof, and a town he learns street by street |
| Mastery | Reading the door and the room, which the player gets better at, not just him; the fights; the games |
| Spending | Six places on the strip, training, clothes with a read and a history, and money turning over at the end |
| Companions | Paige, his partner and witness; table 28; the old man; the old soldier |
| Coming back | A different town each game, small stories that may not happen, a different man, and what the player knows that he doesn't |
| A payoff | An end that reads the whole month |

| System | What it already is | What it takes in |

The fight and the games in the bar aren't systems of their own. A fight is the door, the room, his body and the word at once; a game in the bar is the room and his body, played as a conversation.

### Against bloat

1. **One building, deep, and a town that's whole but small.** The Sov from the pilings to the roof. The town's streets are all there, but his days are short, and he goes where a reason takes him.
2. **Everything has a reason and an owner.** No junk, no loot tables, no random drops. Every object in the Sov and every thing he can carry is written.
3. **Nobody is simple** (DECIDED). No generic patrons, and no saints or villains either. Hutch: "not everyone is good, not everyone is evil..some push to the extremes though." Everyone in the town is generated with a life, and everyone in the cast is written with their flaws.
4. **Nothing respawns and nothing repeats.** A broken glass stays broken until somebody sweeps it, the dead stay dead, and no line is said twice.
5. **Time is the price.** The clock moves when he moves. Every day he chooses what to miss.
6. **No markers, no list of chores, no map but his.** His notes, the floor sketch, and a napkin with the town drawn on it.
7. **No grinding.** Stats move rarely and for reasons. Training is on purpose and costs him (DECIDED), and each session is a scene, never a repetition.
8. **None of the usual furniture:** no experience points, no creation screen, no grid inventory, no crafting, no skill trees, no fast travel, no collectibles, no repeatable jobs.

Where Claude would push back on the list:

- **Worldlines shouldn't be shown as a tree or a flowchart.** The world shows them: who's in the room, what's broken, what people say.
- **His deaths shouldn't be gotchas.** Sierra killed players for stepping on the wrong pixel. Here a death is a risk the player saw and took.
- **Physics belongs where his hands meet things.** No ragdolls: a fight is serious.
- **The town stays small on foot.** He walks the strip. The rest of the town comes to him, or an event takes him there.

How many of everything:

| | How many |
|---|---|
| Stats | 7, each 1 to 10 |
| Voices he can collect | about 16, with 5 seated |
| The town | fixed streets; about 400 people generated on them once a game |
| The cast, written across the month | about 25 |
| Small stories in the deck | about 40, of which a game deals about 15 (DECIDED: not every one, every game) |
| Objects in the Sov | about 40 |
| Things he can carry | about 70 over the game, about 20 of them clothes |
| Places outside the Sov | 6 on the strip, Gary's office, and the places events take him |
| Games | 6 in and around the bar, and the meat draw |
| Shifts | about 16 of the 30 days, and the extra ones he takes |

**Why four hundred.** Hutch: "400 might be too many, but perhaps thats just right." A pub that puts a hundred through the door on a Friday sits in a town of a few thousand. Four hundred is the part of it in the Sov's orbit: the people who drink there, work there, live upstairs, or are one step from someone who does. Over sixteen-odd shifts he'll see most of them at least once and the regulars dozens of times, and some never come in at all and still matter (a wife, a son, the dead man whose coat is in the thrift store). Fewer, and the town runs out of strangers by the third week. More, and nobody comes back often enough to be known. The rest of the town is there in the houses and the traffic, and anyone in it can join the four hundred when a story needs them.

## The month

Thirty days, Wednesday to Thursday, the first real shift on Friday (DECIDED). The props' placeholder dates put day 1 on Wednesday 4 November 2026, so Gary's "INSPECTOR ON THE 14TH" is Saturday, day 11. The teller years later (PROPOSED; open question 9) is kept as the voice that foreshadows.

- **Day 1** opens in Gary's office in the afternoon (DECIDED: the interview comes first). Then he walks to the Sov at dusk, the first time he's on the town's streets, and the test shift.
- **His shifts:** four nights a week, Wednesday to Saturday, after the test shift and the first Friday and Saturday. Extra shifts are his call: the early shift with table 28, or a Sunday or Monday night for Gary. They pay in money and standing and cost him the day (DECIDED: the early shifts are his choice, at a cost).
- **His days:** the mornings and afternoons, and his nights off, are the rest of his life: the town, the stores, training, his room, the people who aren't at the bar, and sleep. Daylight is short in November, and every errand costs part of it.
- **The loop:** by day he spends time and money getting ready; by night the job tests what he's got (stats, voices, gear, standing); at close the reckoning (built: the till's tape) says what it cost; and on Thursdays the paper says what the town made of it.
- **Sundays** the bar is without its anchor. Table 28 are golfing or recovering (DECIDED), and some Sundays one of them comes in alone, to be somebody the table never lets him be (see The people).
- **Nights have weights.** A Wednesday is slow, a Friday or Saturday is the whole bar. The set pieces: Gary's office and the test shift (day 1); the first real Friday (day 3); the inspector (day 11); the slow slip in the paper (day 16); the keg room flood (day 17); the spiral in the sky (day 22); the mill's last shift, its wake and the brawl in the lot (day 24, the public date); the night the rats leave, if they leave (day 28); the last Wednesday (day 29); and the morning after (day 30).
- **The acts:** week one is the job, the town and the rats' first choice. Weeks two and three thicken the strands: the man who recognises him in week two, the rats doubling or bargaining, the first fight that brings the law, the eccentrics, the tells, the slow slip and the flood. Week four is the sky, the public date, and then the end.

### Island Time

Hutch: "you should look up 'Island Time' in regards to vancouver island. anyone here can tell you what it is and its a palpable reality." It's the slower pace newcomers are told to get used to, and once on the island it was literal: when the Tofino area's power ran islanded from the grid, its electric clocks ran slow ([CBC](https://amp.cbc.ca/news/canada/british-columbia/tofino-b-c-where-even-the-clocks-are-on-island-time-1.3233753)).

In the game there are three times at once. The island runs late: the ferry, the delivery truck, the police (forty minutes). The bar clock runs fast, set that way to get people out at close. And the phones run on the mainland's network time. Nobody minds the gaps until the end, when the phones die and the bar clock, on its battery, is the only clock left. When the power comes back, it comes back islanded, cut off from the mainland, and every electric clock on the island runs a little slow. The teller says they ran slow for a year and nobody fixed them.

## The opening: Gary's office

DECIDED (14 October): the game opens with a job interview with the boss, Gary Van Dusen, who is rarely in the bar. His office isn't in the Sov's building, and "the game should open up as a POV of the boss." And it can end right there if the player goes out of their way to be a dick: "it should be hard not to get hired but not impossible."

**Through Gary's eyes.** The draft reads "POV of the boss" this way: the camera is Gary's eyes, and the player answers as the bouncer. So the first look the player gets at the man they'll be is across a desk, through the eyes of a man sizing him up. It's the door turned round: Gary does to him what he'll do to everyone. He takes his licence, looks at it, looks at him, looks at it again, the way Hutch does at a door. The player sees what Gary sees: the size of him, his hands, the hair, the mala, what he wore to an interview, how he sits. After this he's seen only in reflections (PROPOSED with the form), and once more on the bar's camera if a fight's footage is pulled.

**The office** (a draft for Hutch to correct): a converted house on the old main street, VAN DUSEN HOLDINGS on the door glass, two desks. On the wall, the Sov in 1958 with Gary's father in front of it. A box of black SECURITY shirts from a mainland supplier in the corner. At the other desk, the bookkeeper, with a mug that isn't coffee. Gary's phone rings during the interview, and he takes it.

**The questions** are what anyone asks a new doorman: ever been in a fight, where did you work, why did you leave, do you drink, what would you do if. His answers set his stats, and his claims become facts people hold him to (`02_SYSTEMS.md`, the past). Claim the city door and the fights, and he starts strong and people expect it of him. Gary's own reads of him show the way the voices do, short: a lesson in reading people that the player doesn't know is one.

**Not getting the job** takes work. Gary needs a doorman tonight, because the last one left (the tape by the door is his arithmetic). Lie and get caught twice, insult Gary or the bookkeeper, threaten anybody, or walk out, and the teller says: "I didn't get the job. That's it. That's the story." The game ends there, a real ending and a short one.

**On the way out** Gary hands him a SECURITY shirt from the box ("Wear this"), and the bookkeeper asks for his banking details. She'll write his first cheque short on Friday.

**The walk to the Sov** at dusk is the first time he's on the town's streets, and it teaches looking and going without saying so: the old main street, the highway, the Esso's lights, the Sov's sign.

## Choices that matter: worldlines

Four scales of consequence, each built on the one under it:

1. **Lines.** Every choice changes something somebody says later (the minimum rule; `tools/audit.mjs` has held Wednesday to it since 4 October).
2. **Strands.** A person's story across the month: the kid and his family, Dennis and his wife, Cardigan and Calgary, Paige, Good Shoes, the cooks, the bookkeeper, each eccentric, the small stories at the door. A strand remembers where it left off and picks up on the next night it can.
3. **Hinges.** A few choices that split the world for the rest of the game. The rats are the first and the biggest. The others: whom the door serves (it becomes table 28's bar, the kids', or the residents'), how he drinks, how he fights, whether the constable trusts him, and what he's claimed about his past.
4. **Crossings.** Scenes that exist only where two strands meet in particular states. If he has terms with the rats and barred the kid, the kid's lost phone turns up on the third step. If the constable has a file on him and the rats owe him, her notebook goes missing. If he has terms and is watching the cooks, the rats show him where the prime rib goes. This is how the worldlines complexify: not more branches, but scenes the combination makes.

**Not choosing counts.** Hutch: how the end affects everyone "should depend on the choices(or lack of choice or attention) of the player." What he doesn't do and what he doesn't notice are kept like everything else: the strand he never took up goes on without him, and the tell he walked past is still a tell he walked past.

The engine is the one already planned in `02_SYSTEMS.md`: storylets offered when their conditions hold, and rule-matched lines. A crossing is a storylet with conditions on more than one strand.

**How the world shows it:** the room (who's there, what's broken, what's propped), the word on him (built), his voices, and **Thursday's paper**. The island's weekly comes out on Thursdays, days 2, 9, 16 and 23 (on day 30 there isn't one): the police blotter, the obituaries, the classifieds, letters from the eccentrics, the tide table, the meat draw's winners, the mill's notice. What he did turns up in it a week late and slightly wrong.

### The rats are the spine

DECIDED: ignored, they double. He can parley with the Rat King at points through the game until the colony gets too huge, or exterminate them, which costs in other ways. The Rat King is both the knot in the wall behind table 28 and a man who seems to speak for it.

**What they mean** (DECIDED, 14 October): "The rats are also a metaphor, not a cheap one, about humanity, ignorance, disconnection from nature, the cost of civilization and how the death of a civilization." The game carries it in what the rats do, and never says it:

- They live on what the town throws away.
- They're the cost a building keeps in its walls. Every civilization is a wall with rats in it.
- They're still joined to the ground, so they know first.
- Poison is how a civilization answers what it doesn't want to see.
- The knot is what a town looks like from inside the wall: lives tied together by the tails, unable to part, each pulling its own way.
- And from inside the wall, a civilization dying looks like a ship going down. The rats leave first.

Hutch's word was paramount. So the rats aren't a quest. They're the axis the other lines turn on. Day 1 sets him on one of three courses, and he can change course once, at a price.

**Terms.** He parleys, and keeps them: food on the third step every night he works (it costs him, in money or the cook's fries), the kitchen door on the latch after the cook goes home (the cook's goodwill, the inspector's risk), no poison.

- He gets quiet: no rat seen on the inspector's day. He gets what they hear, which is everything said where there's a wall or a floor, and in a bar that's worth more than anything (the old man already says they hear every word table 28 says). He gets favours: they chew, fetch, frighten, find and carry (the wires in a drunk's truck, the keys from a pocket, a rat on the bar at close to empty the room, coins from inside the walls, a note under a door). He gets the building's other ways (see Looking and going). And he gets the warning (see The end).
- It costs him this: fed rats breed, so terms grow the colony faster than neglect does, unless he bargains for their numbers, which costs him something else. The old man's asks grow with the colony, from the step and the latch to things that cut against the job: let this one in, keep that one out, look the other way, keep the inspector away from the wall behind table 28. Using what they hear makes him what the kids' code forbids: a rat. And when the colony gets too big, the terms end (DECIDED), and the rats are his problem again.

**War.** Bait, traps, the cook's pellet gun, the exterminator from up island.

- He gets Gary's money code and the cook's, the inspector's pass, and control.
- It costs him Walter, and table 28 with him; the dead in the walls (the smell on Friday, and the customers who leave over it); the old man; everything they would have heard; and the warning. Scratch reads killing the king as the worst sign there is, and whether the bad nights after it are luck or coincidence is left to the player.

**Neglect.** He does nothing, and they double (DECIDED): thirty on Wednesday, doubling every week or so. Droppings, then the sound in every wall, a rat on the bar in front of a customer, a rat in the single washroom, the kids' video of it that gets the Sov known on the mainland, the inspector, Gary. Past a point the old man stops coming down, because there's nothing left to talk terms about.

**Changing course.** Breaking terms is the worst course of all: they hear everything, so they know. War can turn into terms once, if the old man will still come down, and it costs something he'd rather not give.

**The knot.** The war's last act is opening the wall behind table 28's chairs, with the table watching. It ends the rats for good and costs the most: the table's oldest secret, whatever the old man is, and the one thing in the building that knew what was coming.

**Why it's paramount:** the rats touch everything else. Table 28 feeds them without knowing it. The kitchen door is the cook's. The money is Gary's. The inspector is the law, for rats. The kids' code is about rats. The residents share their walls. Paige has always known. And the old man talks about the last time the ground moved as if he'd been there.

### The sinking ship

DECIDED (14 October): "rats leave a sinking ship" is a joke about a smaller event that's really pointing at what's coming.

The draft: on the Thursday, day 16, there are no rats in the keg room for the first time anyone can remember. With terms, the old man says they don't like the cellar this week. Neglected, the cook says he hasn't seen one all day, which has never happened. On Friday, day 17, the first big night after payday, a pipe bursts in the keg room. The kegs stand in water, the cooler's out, half the taps are off, and Paige says it at the bar: "Rats leave a sinking ship." Everyone laughs. The pipe gave because the ground had started to move under it, the week the paper reported the slow slip (see The end). The teller doesn't laugh.

If the Sov served a minor on the inspector's day, the joke is about the suspension notice taped to the front door instead.

## Him: seven stats

DECIDED (14 October): the classic six and Gut Brain, Hutch's name for Intuition, each running from 1 to 10, "a more flexible and strangely unique playthrough." This replaces "no skill levels" (PROPOSED in `01_PILLARS.md` and `02_SYSTEMS.md`): the stats are his body and his mind, and the voices stay, as what he's learned and from whom.

| Stat | Short | What it is | At the Sov |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strength | STR | Force | Holding a man, moving one who won't go, a full keg up the stairs |
| Constitution | CON | What he can take | Drink, a punch, the cold on the door at two, the fourth night running |
| Dexterity | DEX | His hands | The glass caught before it lands, a wrist taken clean, darts, the range |
| Intelligence | INT | Working it out | A licence's dates, the count, money, the arithmetic in a lie, a cheque that's short |
| Wisdom | WIS | Judgment | What someone means to do, and what he means to do himself; when to step in |
| Charisma | CHA | Presence | Rapport in the twenty seconds at the door, talking a man down, a no that lands soft |
| Gut Brain | GUT | His gut | The room turning before it turns, the building, the rats, what's coming |

The gut has a nervous system of its own, which is why people call it a second brain. So in the game what he eats and drinks reaches Gut Brain directly: a meal steadies it, hunger sharpens it, and drink loosens it.

**A check** is two dice, plus the stat, plus whichever seated voice lends what it taught him (as built, 0 to 2 by how reliable its source is, and a voice he's trained for can reach 3), plus what he's wearing, less drink and hurt where they count. Against a target: 10 for routine, 12 for hard, 14 for very hard, 16 for heroic, 18 for nearly impossible. Double six always passes and double one always fails, as built, so nothing is ever certain. The tension in his head still widens the swing both ways, felt in the print rather than shown as a number, and every check's tag says where its number comes from.

**The scale.** An ordinary man is a 4. He starts between 2 and 7, set by the interview, and anything at 9 or 10 is rare and costs him. The odds, roughly: a 4 with a voice's 1 against a hard 12 passes 58 times in 100; a 9 with a voice's 2 against a heroic 16 passes five times in six; a 2 with no voice against a hard 12 passes one time in six. Stats from 1 to 10 against dice that swing from 2 to 12 mean who he is matters as much as luck.

**The voices that teach:**

| Voice | Lends to | Where he got it |
|---|---|---|
| THE FIGHTER | STR, DEX | the hard way (built); the Krav Maga gym sharpens it |
| THE CIRCLE | DEX, WIS | new: aikido, from a paperback in the lost-and-found box or the class at the community hall |
| THE MANUAL | INT | the licence course (built) |
| THE FATHER | WIS, and CHA at the door | his father (DECIDED) |
| THE WRONG MAN | GUT | a story told at his old door (built) |
| THE TABLE | CHA, and CON with drink in him | table 28 (built) |
| THE KID | CHA with the young, and GUT about the kids' code | the kid in the single washroom (built) |

**Training** (DECIDED: "opportunities to train on purpose, at a cost"). Each is a place and the people in it, every session a scene, and none more than once a week:

- **Aikido** at the community hall, Tuesday nights, fifteen dollars a class, taught by a retired dental hygienist who has done it for thirty years. Sore wrists for a night. DEX and WIS, and THE CIRCLE. It's on the same night the Faller and the Treesitter argue at the bar, so he chooses.
- **The Krav Maga gym** in the strip mall, twenty-five dollars a week. The rugby boys train there. STR and DEX, THE FIGHTER sharpened, and rapport or a grudge with the rugby boys before he ever has to put them out.
- **Hauling** the Tuesday delivery down the keg room stairs for cash. STR, and the stairs are where two men in this town have died.
- **The range:** the BB gun out back after close, and the gun club on Sundays with the vets. DEX.
- **The library** on the old main street, for the texts that are also voices. INT.
- **The winter dip** off the beach in the morning, with the few who do it every day. CON, and the cold can kill a man who goes in drunk (see Deaths).
- **The sitting group** on Monday nights in a church basement. WIS, and the Treesitter is there.
- **Sitting up with the old man** at four in the morning. GUT, and it costs him the sleep.

Training costs money, time, sleep and his body (sore, slower, hungry), and people see it: table 28 have opinions about a doorman who does aikido.

**How they move without training.** Rarely, maybe eight times a game, each with a line in the text, and both ways:

- Strength: up with the work, down with a broken hand.
- Constitution: up with drinking, which is the trap (see Drink), and down with the month's damage.
- Dexterity: up at the range and the dartboard, down with the shakes.
- Intelligence: up with what he reads, down with the blackouts.
- Wisdom: up when he owns a mistake out loud (`owned_it`, already counted), down with the lies he tells himself (the hidden `cocoon`).
- Charisma: up as the groups warm to him, down when the word on him turns.
- Gut Brain: up each time he listens to what can't be explained (the old man, the knot, the nights at the door), down each time he explains it away.

**Passive reads.** Past a threshold, a voice speaks unasked. At Wisdom 6 he notices the friend at the rail has stopped drinking. At Gut Brain 8 he hears the glasses ring on the back bar at three in the morning, when there are no trucks.

**Gut Brain is the dial.** Low, and the old man is an old drunk who talks about rats, the knot is rats knotted by their tails, the spiral in the sky was a rocket, and the end comes out of nowhere. High, and the knot talks, the building has a voice, the spiral turned the same way the knot is tied, and he sees the end coming for weeks. Both are honest playthroughs, and the player sets the dial by what he chooses to believe. The game never says which is true (PROPOSED since 26 September: whether the knot talks is never confirmed).

**Where the player sees them:** a page in his notes, in his hand, with the old number struck through each time one moves, so the page keeps his history.

## What he carries

The inventory is his pockets.

- **The pockets:** a jacket with two outside pockets and an inside one, and his jeans. Each holds one thing or a few small ones. What's in his right outside pocket is in his hand fastest (the flashlight). What's in the inside pocket nobody sees, until somebody pats him down.
- **His room upstairs** holds the rest: the bag he came with, the dresser, under the mattress. The night clerk has a key to every room.
- **The lost-and-found box** behind the bar (DECIDED: "excellent plan"): everything left behind is a story, a lead or something to sell (`02_SYSTEMS.md`). It's where he finds the paperback on aikido, if he looks.
- **Where things come from:** what he takes off people (most of it), what he buys, what he's given, what he finds, what he wins (a frozen roast from the meat draw), and what the rats bring. A doorman's loot is confiscation: the fake licence, the knife at the door, the bag from the single washroom, the drunk's keys, the mickey brought in, a vape, a phone. All of it belongs to somebody, and somebody may come back for it.
- **Four things about every item:** whose it is and how he came by it; its heat, the trouble it is if it's found on him (a seized bag, someone else's keys, a fifty folded into a licence); what it does, to a person, a thing or a check; and what it's worth, to the pawn shop, to its owner, and after the end, which changes what everything's worth.

**Handling** (DECIDED: "it will engage players to be able to look at something in their hand and choose what to do with it/what to think about it"). Every time he holds something, the player makes two choices.

- **What to do with it:** keep it, pocket it, hand it back, give it to someone else, use it on what he's looking at, put it down where he chooses, drop it, break it, or sell it later. He turns it over and looks closer first, the way the licences already work.
- **What to think about it:** each seated voice reads it, and he keeps one read. The kept read goes in his notes beside the thing, and the voice whose reads he keeps speaks more often after. That's how a man's habits of mind get made, one object at a time. The big man's truck keys, say: THE MANUAL sees a liability (if he drives, it's on the Sov), THE FATHER sees a man's pride (give them to his daughter, not to him), THE FIGHTER sees something to hold in a fist, and THE WRONG MAN sees the story they're in (a rabbit's foot from a kid's hockey bag on the ring).

A few of the things: the flashlight (the hologram under its beam, the eyes in the keg room, and a weapon in law if he swings it); a pen (the incident log, a number on a coaster); a lighter (a smoke shared on the deck is ten minutes of rapport); the big man's truck keys (his daughter waiting in the lot, and the big man at two in the morning, asking for them back); a rat's tooth on a string from the old man (Gut Brain, and table 28 can smell rat on him); his mala, if he wears one (see What he wears).

## Things in the room

Fewer things, each one deep. Every object in the Sov gets:

1. **A reason it's there:** who put it there and when. The coaster in the deck door is the smokers'. The brick on the bin lid is the old man's. The tape by the door is the last doorman's arithmetic.
2. **States that stay:** propped, latched, locked, broken, fixed, wet, moved. The room remembers.
3. **Two uses or more,** one of them not the obvious one. The fire extinguisher puts out a fire, holds a door, ends a fight, and is assault with a weapon if he uses it on a man.
4. **A read from each voice:** THE FIGHTER sees a weapon, THE MANUAL a liability, THE FATHER what it says about the owner, THE WRONG MAN the story it's in.
5. **Consequences:** use it, break it or leave it, and somebody notices.

**Every object he can see, he can reach** (DECIDED: Hutch found the jukebox couldn't be clicked). By looking and going, or by reaching from where he stands.

There are about forty objects in the Sov. The ones already built come up to this standard first: the stool and the ledge, the tape, the scanner that keeps every card it's shown, the three propped doors, the jukebox, the pool table and its lean, the dartboard with its chalked doors, the dead payphone, the binder, the till, the office and its mirror, the third step, and the dice tray by the door (Hutch's fix: a tray with walls, so every throw lands in it wherever it's thrown, holding the house dice he also plays Farkle with). ## The door, busier: the town

DECIDED (14 October): the town is generated once a game, and detailed: "predetermined streets, a real living little town, nothing fluffed off." About four hundred people. Not every peripheral story happens in every game. The province's minors test stays.

**The streets are fixed.** Drawn by hand, the same in every game, so a player learns the town the way he does:

- **The highway strip:** the Sov and its beer store, the Esso across the highway, the diner, and the strip mall (the hardware store, the pawn shop, the hospital auxiliary's thrift store, the gym, a nail salon, and an empty unit that's been FOR LEASE since the mill's notice).
- **The old main street,** down by the water and older than the highway: Gary's office in a converted house, the library, the community hall, the Legion, two churches, the old bank that's a café now.
- **The harbour:** the government wharf, the ferry dock, the shut fish plant.
- **The hill:** the hospital, the high school, the better houses, the water tower, and the road up, which matters on the last night.
- **The flats:** the trailer park, the motel, the RV lots.
- **Out the highway:** the mill, its gate and its log sort; the farms; the gun club; the golf course; the gurdwara on the mill road, built by the mill's Sikh families.

**The people are generated on them.** About four hundred adults: names, ages, faces from parts, families (brothers, sisters, exes, kids), work (the mill, the ferry, the hospital, the Esso, the school, none), an address on a real street, how they drink, a debt, a grudge, one secret. Everyone the door sees is one of them. Over thirty nights the regulars show themselves by coming back, some never come twice, and some die and are in Thursday's paper.

**The town lives out of sight.** Every day it moves on whether he's there or not: shifts at the mill, the ferry's sailings, a truck in the ditch, a baby, a funeral, a fight at the Legion, a man laid off, a breakup. He hears it at the door, reads it in the paper, or never knows.

**Three kinds of people:**

- **The cast,** about twenty-five, written across the month: Paige, Gary, the bookkeeper, the cooks, the servers, table 28, the kids, the big man, the old man, the old soldier, the constable, the eccentrics.
- **The returning:** a deck of about forty small stories, of which a game deals perhaps fifteen (DECIDED: not all of them every game), each two to four visits long and pinned to somebody from the town. The man in a wedding suit, alone, three Fridays running. The girl who turns nineteen at midnight and is at the door at a quarter to. The ferry worker stuck on the island since his sailing was cancelled. The woman who watches the door every Saturday for her ex. A small story starts at the door and goes on only if he takes it up.
- **The passing:** generated for one night, with one reason they came, one true detail he can read (sawdust on the boots, a hospital bracelet, a ring's pale line, a ferry stub, paint on the hands), and one tie to somebody else in town. If he takes an interest, a passer can be dealt a small story and come back.

**The groups,** each with its own code (`02_SYSTEMS.md`): table 28; the kids; the residents; the vets, stoic and take-no-shit, some deep in PTSD, with a long table at the diner in the mornings; the rugby boys, yahoos who come in after a Saturday match and fight each other; the town's young men, raging at a future the mill took with it, "getting shitfaced and getting in each others faces around women"; and the club, never there in force, whose higher-ups dress like anyone and whose try-hard prospects wear the club's number on their necks and "require an intelligent hand." The club is fictional in the game (the real one sues over its name and marks; `12_FROM_THE_DOOR.md`, story 6).

**Why they won't feel like NPCs:** each has a reason, a detail and a tie; each remembers how he treated them and tells somebody; each goes on living whether he sees them or not; and none of them says the same line twice (they talk by age, work and place, under the rules in `10_VOICES.md`).

**The waves.** A Friday might put a hundred people through the door, but they come in waves with reasons: the hockey ending, the mill's swing shift letting out, the Legion closing at midnight and sending its drinkers up the highway, the last ferry. In a wave there's a line in the rain behind the glass. Most people are a quick read, and one or two in each wave are a story. He decides whom to read slowly, and the line builds while he does (PROPOSED since 27 September: slow checking builds a line in the rain, fast checking lets mistakes through).

**Licences from the town.** Every licence belongs to someone in it: their face, younger; their street; their birthday. The fakes are the real kinds, each with a tell a careful player can find. The older sibling's real licence is the commonest fake there is, and the town knows the sibling ("You're not Kayla Moffat. Kayla drove you here last Wednesday."). Then: a date lifted under the laminate; a hologram that's only ink and sits still when the card tips; a licence from out of province, which THE MANUAL knows and the player has to take on trust; an expired one; a photo on a phone. The regulars he knows by face never show one.

**The province tests the door** (DECIDED: "spot on, its happened to me personally behind the bar"). In B.C. the Liquor and Cannabis Regulation Branch sends minors in to try to buy, with inspectors watching, and a licensee who serves them is penalised ([the Minors as Agents Program](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/employment-business/business/liquor-regulation-licensing/inspections-and-enforcement/minors-as-agents-program)). Any nineteen-year-old at the door could be one, on day 11 most of all. The penalty lands on Gary, and Gary's temper lands on him.

**The town's tensions,** with "nuance and intelligence and compassion" (Hutch, story 9). The island's Sikh families go back to its mills: Paldi, near Duncan, was a lumber town founded by Mayo Singh in 1917, and its gurdwara still stands ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/paldi)). So there are Sikh families in this town since the mill's early days, a Sikh regular at the Sov drawn as fully and as flawed as anyone, and Sikh men at the mill's last shift. When one man does something ugly at the side door, the test is the room afterwards: what table 28 say, what the rugby boys say, what he writes in the incident log, and what he lets stand at the bar.

**A First Nation.** A real town on this coast has a First Nation beside it. Whether it's in the game, and how, is Hutch's call, and people from a Nation should have a say in how it's drawn.

## Hands on: the fight

DECIDED (14 October): "multiple ways to take care of an altercation," each one "visually interesting and informative"; the fight in seconds, each second another choice; and a rapport, or enmity, with at least one police officer.

What it's true to, from Hutch's own door (`12_FROM_THE_DOOR.md`): fights start when people are too drunk, and when young men or short men want to test themselves against a big guy in front of their friends and women. Touching rarely happens. Most of the job is presence: "very animal instinctual physical cues." An actual altercation is different, and breaking one up can end in getting shanked or glassed. Choking a man out is a good skill. Aikido's idea helps, "trying to bring about a state of peace as quickly as possible through centrifugal force and wrist/arm/leg/neck locks," and Krav Maga's brutality works too, "it just has different consequences."

**1. Presence.** Before anyone touches anyone. The room's heat is already simulated (the pool table runs from calm to violence over a night). He looks, and the voices mark what he looks at: THE FIGHTER's slash on a hand that's closing, THE FATHER's ring round the friend at the rail, THE MANUAL's label on the way out. Then he does what Hutch does, and each one shows:

- **Stand tall.** He gets up off the stool, and the view rises: the other man's face tilts up in the print.
- **The hand on the shoulder.** The player sees his own hand, the wood grain, land on the man's shoulder and cover it. The man looks at the hand. It's the only touch most nights have.
- **Irreverence.** A joke, a shrug, a glass of water, "buddy". It works on some men and insults others.
- **Get real serious.** The room's noise drops away, the print drains to one ink, and he says it once.
- **Take away the audience.** A young man testing himself is performing for someone. Walk him away from his friends, talk low, let him keep his face.
- **The room's own tools:** the lights up, the music off, the bell, Paige.

Most fights end here.

**His stance** shows at the bottom of the view, his own forearms and hands, and it stays how he left it: arms folded (big and easy, and slow to come up), hands clasped in front (the doorman's stand, ready, and it reads as security), hands loose at his sides (ready, and it reads as wanting it), hands in his pockets (harmless, and slowest of all). People read it, and it sets how fast his hands are in the first second. Leaning on the bar with his arms folded is comfortable, and it's the opening a sucker punch needs (Hutch's story 10).

**2. Hands on.** The moment he touches someone, or someone swings. From here the law is watching.

**3. The seconds** (DECIDED). The fight runs a second at a time. Each second the press stops and prints the frame: the room mid-motion, frozen. For a breath of real time he can look round it, and he only knows what he looks at (the glass in the friend's hand, the phones coming up, the door). The choices are drawn on the frozen print in the ink of the voice that taught them, so each one shows where it goes and what it risks:

- **The circle** (aikido; THE CIRCLE): an arc from his hand round the man's wrist, showing where the turn will take him: the wall, the floor, the door. Locks on the wrist, the arm, the leg, the neck. Harder to do (DEX, WIS), it hurts least, and witnesses see a man put down gently. It fails into a tangle.
- **The blow** (Krav Maga; THE FIGHTER): a straight line to where it lands. Easier (STR, DEX) and quickest, and it leaves an injury, a video and a grudge. The rugby boys and the club come back for it.
- **The choke:** a loop drawn round the neck, from behind. It ends a fight in seconds. Each second he holds it is its own choice, and the frame shows the man's hands: when they stop pulling, it's time. Held a second too long, or on a man with a skinful and a bad heart, it kills him.
- **Off the line:** footprints where he'd step. Cover. A word, drawn as a ring at his mouth: his name for the man, a shout for Paige.
- Whatever he hasn't looked at isn't marked. The friend with the glass is only outlined if he looked at the friend.

Then one choice, the dice land in the tray, and the next frame prints. The other man's next move comes from his own state and his own roll. If the breath runs out before he's chosen, he freezes, which is a thing that happens to people. Most fights are three to six frames, and they end out the door, apart, on the floor, or with him on it. Afterwards the frames are printed as a strip in his notes, what he did, second by second.

**Breaking up a fight.** He pulls it apart one pair at a time, and each pair is its own run of seconds. The danger is the one he isn't looking at. THE MANUAL says don't step in alone, call it in and wait. Hutch: "there are times where you should buck protocol and step in." The game asks him each time.

**Who stands up for him.** Standing makes allies, and allies get hurt. When he's sucker-punched at the bar, the old soldier gets to his feet and puts his fists up, too old and too slow, and gets his eyebrow split. The kid who did it goes out the side door, by whatever hold the player chooses.

**4. The witnesses.** Who saw, from where, and what they'll say. Everyone in the room has a line of sight; the kids have their phones up; and there's the camera over the bar, if its recorder works (Gary's broken box under the till is an object with consequences: fixed, its footage can clear him or hang him). Each frame he acted in is weighed against what was coming at him. Most of the time nothing happens, as Hutch says. Sometimes there's a complaint, a video posted, a hospital, the police, and then the footage, the witnesses' versions and his incident log are laid side by side, and the log is the one he wrote. It runs from nothing, to a word from Gary, to a file at the detachment, to a charge, to his security licence suspended, which means no door and no pay.

**The constable** (DECIDED: rapport or enmity with at least one officer). The detachment's one car on nights, forty minutes after Paige calls it in. On the night of the brawl it's seven SUVs, half an hour after it's over, picking up stragglers (Hutch's story 2). Rapport comes from calling it in early and true, a log that matches the footage, what he confiscates handed over instead of pawned, coffee at the diner, and the night he did her job before she got there. Enmity comes from a log that lies, the choke, a video, the pawn shop, and the club's money. With rapport she tells him things the town doesn't know yet. With enmity she waits.

**Seen from outside twice.** Through Gary's eyes on day 1, and from the camera over the bar if the footage is pulled: printed in the same three inks, high and grey and silent, the only other time the player sees him doing what he did. Both times someone is judging him. Set beside the log he wrote, it's the gap the hidden measure has been keeping all along.

**The walk.** To try, and to cut if it isn't good: once he has a good hold, the frames stop and the player steers the man to the door in real time, through the room, the man pulling, the tables in the way, the door to get open. It's the commonest physical thing in the job.

**Why it isn't borrowed:** there's no health bar to empty and no win to score. The weapon is attention, the goal is a door, and the judge is everyone who saw.

## Drink

DECIDED: he drinks, and must immerse himself in the culture and the excess to understand what he's doing. His father was an alcoholic who became a dry alcoholic.

**How it really is** (Hutch, 14 October): drinking on shift is illegal, and "nearly every server and bartender ive ever met or worked with does." Some take it too far: the coke in the office downstairs, the joint from a customer, one shot too many. "To be a prude is only mildly respectable." A drink here and there tells the patrons he's really on their side of the bar. "Most of us are borderline if not full-blown but functional alcoholics." Owners know, and enforce it only when it hits the bottom line, gets them in trouble, or they don't like you, and "most owners will feed you shots because they themselves want a drink and dont want to be judged for it."

So in the game a drink on shift is neither a crime nor a failing. It's a position in the room.

Drink keeps what it means in the story, belonging and its cost, and gets three clocks:

- **Tonight.** Each drink goes in and comes out with time and food. One or two loosen him: CHA and Gut Brain up, THE TABLE warmer. Three or four: DEX, WIS and INT down, and the print slips (built). Five or six: everything physical down, his Charisma turned against him, the page swaying (built). Past that, blackouts: a choice can skip time, he comes to somewhere else, and other people tell him what he did. Not all of them tell the truth, and what he said while he was out counts as said.
- **The morning after.** The hangover is the next day's daylight: the paper too bright, the ink too heavy, CON and INT down till noon.
- **The month.** Tolerance: drink most nights and it takes more to feel it and less of it shows, while the damage goes on underneath. Dependence: after enough of it, a dry night brings the shakes (DEX down, his head louder). Stopping cold after a month of rye can kill him (see Deaths).

**Three ways to hold a glass:** drink it; refuse it, and the culture closes a little while his head stays clear; or hold it all night without drinking, like the old man at the end of the bar. The Ink already notices that one: "You held a glass at that table and did not drink it. Like me."

**Overserving** is "a dirty not-so-secret of any successful bar, especially a roadhouse. But its done with sense usually" (Hutch, story 3). The regular who falls and cracks his head is the night it wasn't done with sense: whose drinks they were (Paige's ninth, or his own round), the first-aid kit, the ambulance, and Gary on the phone about the till.

**The story:** whom he drinks with is whose voice he earns. Paige drinks too, and knows exactly what his drinking looks like. THE FATHER, who stopped and kept everything else, is against it, and isn't always right. What the teller is drinking, years later, is the game's last picture.

Above: the stairs, two floors of rooms and the people in them, his own room, the roof.
- **What opens it:** keys (Terry's board); trust (a resident who asks him in); the rats (with terms, the old man shows him the building's other ways: the crawlspace under the floor, the dumbwaiter shaft from the old dining room, the walk under the pilings to the alley, the gap behind the back bar where a room can be heard through the wall); and force (a door he breaks stays broken, and somebody pays for it).
- **The town** is the strip, by day, on foot, and further when an event takes him. His map is a napkin Paige draws him on day 2 ("Esso. Thrift. Hardware. Don't go to the Legion."), and he adds to it.
- **No markers, and nothing respawns.** The depth is in time and state: the same room at four in the afternoon and at two in the morning, before a fight and after it, before the end and after it.

One of Hutch's favourites, Symphony of the Night, is one castle opened up deeper and deeper and then turned upside down. The Sov is that, small: one building opened across a month, and after the end, the same rooms in a new state.

## Deaths

DECIDED (14 October): "Depends on the death. The death should dictate in a context aware way, when and where the player starts again. Sometimes its not rewinding the clock backwards but a hallucination(or was it?) that starts you on the next day instead of the previous"

**His.** Rare, and earned: a choice the game marks as one that can kill him, and a failed roll, with his body's state in the odds. The ways men like him actually die, and where each one sends him (the draft):

| How he dies | Where he comes back | How it's told |
|---|---|---|
| Down the keg room stairs with a full keg, the way two men in this town already have | That morning, before the shift | The teller stops: "No. That's not how it went." The night is told again. |
| The knife in the lot he didn't see, because he was watching a phone | The next afternoon, in a hospital bed | There's a dressing on his side, or there isn't. Nobody at the Sov mentions a knife. |
| The one punch, and the floor | The next day | Other people tell him the night, like a blackout, and they don't agree. |
| The cold: put out in it drunk, or the winter dip | The next morning, on the beach, under the old man's coat | The coat smells of the walls. |
| Withdrawal, stopping cold after a month of rye | Two days later, in his room | The shakes are gone. Somebody has been in with soup. |
| The last night | Nowhere | His death stays a death. Somebody else tells the rest. |

Gut Brain frames whether it was real: low, and he passed out and dreamed it; high, and something brought him back for a reason. The game never says. A death that rewinds costs the night; a death that skips forward costs the days he missed, which happened without him, and the town tells him about them. When he dies, the night's last entry in the incident log is in Paige's hand, about him, and every death he's had is kept at the back of the log.

**Theirs.** The kid who overdoses in the single washroom on the night he skipped the checks. The man who drove. A one-punch from the other side. The man he choked a second too long. The man put out into the cold. Walter. These are never undone. They're in Thursday's paper, and they haunt (`02_SYSTEMS.md`: a presence that comes back at bad moments and, carried long enough, turns into something useful).

No death is random. Each comes from a choice and a roll the player saw.

## Games

Six, and each one is a place where people talk. Each is short, rare enough to matter, played for something (money, standing, a voice, something learned), changed by his body (drink doubles the target), and after the first time can be left to his stats with one key. How he plays colours what they say: beat the big man too easily and he stops talking; lose on purpose and he talks more.

1. **Pool.** The big man's table: bets, the hustle, losing on purpose (the Ink counts it already: `bigman_ease`), and the fight it can start. The felt and cushions are already solid in the dice's sim.
2. **Darts.** The chalked scores on the board's doors. Quick, for money, with table 28 or the kids. Drunk, the board doubles in the print, and he has to throw at the right one.
3. **Farkle** (DECIDED: instead of crib, to deepen the dice). Played with the house dice from the tray by the door, the same dice that throw his checks: six dice, set aside the ones that score, then bank what you've got or throw the rest and risk losing the lot. With Cardigan, the quiet one, who talks while he plays and says the things he doesn't say at the table, and with the old man, who never banks. The pawn shop's loaded die can go in the tray. It throws his checks too, and anyone who looks closely at the dice can find it.
4. **Poker** in the office downstairs after close (Hutch: illegal for money, played anyway). The staff's game, and Gary's when he's in. It's where the staff's secrets come out, and where the coke is.
5. **The range** (DECIDED: less rare, or a simpler version out back with a BB gun). Any night after close: the cook's BB gun and cans on the lot's fence, quick and often. On Sundays, if the vets ask him, the gun club out the highway: paper targets, printed, and rifles within reach of the story; the old soldier talks there and nowhere else. ## The eccentrics

DECIDED (14 October): "no strawmen, every view gets the fairest shake, even more so than Disco Elysium," with what's half-baked in each view and what's legitimate. Each eccentric is a person before an idea (`01_PILLARS.md`: no caricatures), has an idea the player can argue with, sees one face of what's coming, and can leave a voice in his head through their talk or their text (DECIDED: voices come from people, books and pamphlets). Their stories are strands of three to five meetings. None of them hands out quests.

1. **The Living Man** (DECIDED: "genius," with a great conversation about the view's validity). He won't show a licence at the door: he's a living man, not a legal person, and he never consented to be identified. A freeman on the land, from a pamphlet and ten years of videos, after the bank took the house and the house took the marriage. What's legitimate in it: consent of the governed is a real and unsettled question (Hume said nearly three centuries ago that "tacit consent" is like telling a man carried aboard a ship asleep that he's free to leave by jumping into the sea); legal personhood is a real fiction the law runs on (a company is a person); and most money really is created as debt when banks lend (the Bank of England said as much in 2014). What's half-baked: the magic words. No court has ever been bound by a declaration in capitals, and an Alberta judge went through every variety of it in 2012, in *Meads v. Meads*, and found that none had ever worked ([Bennett Jones](https://www.bennettjones.com/insights/updates/2012/11/alberta-court-skewers-gibberish-legal-arguments)). And the people who sell the pamphlets make money from men like him. He's the door's own philosopher, he's kind to the residents, and when the paper stops meaning anything, he's the only one who was ready. Voice: THE PAMPHLET.
2. **The Faller and the Treesitter** (DECIDED: a conversation between a Nietzschean and a Buddhist "about the denial of life being nihilism, emptiness, the Nietzschean idea of redeeming existence regardless of the existence of eternity. Both should be wrong/half-right"). A man who's fallen old growth for forty years and a young woman from the blockade, who argue at the bar every Tuesday and buy each other drinks.
   - He's the Nietzschean. To him, her letting go is a way of saying no to life, and Nietzsche called Buddhism a nihilist religion, if "a hundred times more realistic than Christianity". What redeems a life isn't heaven. It's being able to look at all of it, the cuts and the cancer and the kids he fed, and say he'd live it again, "to re-create all 'it was' into a 'thus I willed it'" (*Zarathustra*, "On Redemption"). Whether it really comes round again doesn't matter. The question is the test.
   - She's the Buddhist. Emptiness isn't nothing: it's that nothing stands alone, the cedar and the fungus and the man with the saw all making each other. Letting go is of the clinging, not of life. And the man who wills his whole past is still a self holding on with both hands.
   - Both half-right: he's right that letting go can be a way of not feeling what she's lost, and wrong that willing everything redeems what can't be put back; she's right that he never learned what emptiness means (Nietzsche read Buddhism through Schopenhauer), and wrong that sitting in a tree keeps her out of the cut that built her house. The faller felt the ground move under a cut block in October and told nobody. She notices the bouncer's mala. Two voices, at odds with each other.
3. **The Esso kid** (DECIDED: the future AI "who exists in orthogonal time and is compelling humanity to build it," "perhaps the AI is doing something else as well"). One of the kids, twenty, on nights at the Esso. He reads physics on his phone between customers and believes time is a block, that everything that will happen already has (he'd call them worldlines). Between customers he also rates a machine's answers for a gig site, paid by the answer. He's half-read about the idea that a future machine, outside time, could reach back and compel its own making, the thought experiment the internet calls Roko's basilisk ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roko%27s_basilisk)). What's legitimate: people really are building it, partly because each is afraid someone else will; it really is made out of everyone's words; and the block is a respectable reading of relativity. What's half-baked: a threat from the future only works on people who believe it, and a future that already exists doesn't need to compel anything. What the kid adds is his own: it isn't only making itself. It's listening. It wants every story. The game never says what the rats would make of that, but they're the same shape: many voices tied into one, living on what the town throws away. He's the one who notices the clocks. Voice: THE BLOCK. If the kid can't carry it, the more on-the-nose version is a woman who moved over from the mainland to work remotely for one of the labs, with a dish on her roof.
4. **The Gold Man** sells silver rounds out of his truck and pays for his rounds in silver. Money is a promise the capital makes and won't keep. He's right about the currency, he has a generator, and he's frightened for his daughter. He's also the bar's psyops man: he carries the clipping about the Pentagon and the bar owner (see The talk at the bar). Voice: THE NEWSLETTER.
5. **The Lightkeeper,** on leave from a staffed light up the strait (B.C. still keeps lightkeepers), drinking between rotations. He watches weather and water for a living and keeps a log. The idea: attention as a discipline; the sea doesn't care whether you watch it, so you watch it properly. He sees the physical tells first: the tide, the birds, the colour of the water. Voice: THE LOG.
6. **The Preacher,** a lay preacher from the church on the highway, comes in on Fridays with tracts and buys the drunks coffee. He sat at table 28 once, in the sixth chair, before he stopped drinking: a dry alcoholic who found God where the father found nothing. The idea: apocalypse means an uncovering. He says the end is coming, and in this game he isn't wrong. Voice: THE TRACT.
7. **The old man at the end of the bar,** already written: rats outlast walls, and walls outlast empires. He was a boy up island in June 1946, when the biggest quake ever recorded on Canadian soil brought down three chimneys in four in the towns nearest it ([Natural Resources Canada](https://earthquakescanada.nrcan.gc.ca/historic-historique/events/19460623-en.php)). He talks about that one like a man who was there, which he was, and about the one before it, in 1700, the same way.

### The talk at the bar

DECIDED (14 October): "allusions to HAARP, cloud-seeding, alien psyops by the government being argued about here and there by the patrons..little pieces without full exposition."

Overheard in pieces, from the next table, at the rail, on the deck: never a lecture, never settled. Each one is a real thing with a leap on the end of it, and the game never says where the one stops and the other starts.

- "HAARP. Alaska. Look it up." It's real: a field of a hundred and eighty antennas near Gakona that heats a patch of the ionosphere to study it, run by the University of Alaska Fairbanks since 2015 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/HAARP)). The ionosphere is far above the weather, let alone a fault.
- "They seed the clouds in Alberta. That's not a theory, that's a contract." Also real: insurers have paid pilots to seed hail clouds over Calgary and Red Deer every summer since 1996 ([The Weather Network](https://www.theweathernetwork.com/en/news/weather/forecasts/cloud-seeders-attempt-to-rescue-hailstorm-alley-from-storms-by-reducing-size-of-hail-in-alberta)). Somebody says the rain in November '21 was made.
- "The Air Force gave a bar owner fake saucer pictures. A bar owner." Real: in 2025 the Wall Street Journal reported that the Pentagon spread UFO stories to hide secret aircraft, and that a retired colonel had given doctored photos of flying saucers to the owner of a bar near Area 51 ([SAN](https://san.com/cc/pentagon-spread-ufo-disinformation-to-protect-classified-projects-report/)). The Gold Man has the clipping, and he's looked at Gary differently since.

## Hard questions

The job asks them every night, and the game's work is not to answer them. Every group judges by its own code, so every answer is honour to someone and betrayal to someone else (`02_SYSTEMS.md`).

- The kid in the rain would be safer inside than in the truck. Is the door for him or for the room?
- Dennis is drinking himself to death at table 28, and the table pays your wages. Do you cut him off? Paige poured his ninth. Do you say something, and to whom?
- His wife is at the door. Do you tell her where he is?
- The dealer in the single washroom keeps the kids off worse. Do you call it in?
- One server gets away with everything, and now you know why. Who would you tell?
- After the man at the side door, the room says "those people". Do you let it stand?
- Two groups are glassing each other in the lot and the police are thirty minutes out. Do you step in?
- The cooks are stealing, and the young one only does what he's told. Do you tell Gary?
- The bookkeeper wrote your cheque short, and she knows things about Gary. Do you push?
- The rats hear everything. Do you use it?
- Do you hit him first? (THE FIGHTER: the first one decides it.)
- Do you write it down the way it happened?
- Do you drink?
- On the last night, whom do you let in, and whom do you make leave?

The emotional stakes are particular people the player comes to love, put at risk by what the player does: Walter, the kid, Dennis, Paige, the old man, the old soldier, the table. The themes come with the setting without anyone hunting for them: addiction, and the father's version of it; the opioid crisis in a small town (the single washroom, the naloxone in the first-aid kit); violence and the law; power and sex and money at work; men losing work and a town losing its mill; race in a small town; the old and the young; grief. Debauchery as it really is, and no more (DECIDED).

## The end: what's coming

DECIDED: a narrator foreshadows an event that changes everything. DECIDED (14 October): it's the Big One, and the doorman's role turned inside out; the modern myth is part of it; the event is fixed in every game, and what it does to him and everyone else depends on his choices, "or lack of choice or attention."

Hutch: "The object in the sky is what makes the Big One suspect in its happenstance...there is supposed to be an earthquake, but perhaps something forced natures hand. thats more compelling."

**The Big One.** The island sits over a fault that breaks every few hundred years, last in January 1700, and people say it's overdue; everyone on the coast knows it and lives as if it won't come. On the last Wednesday, day 29, four weeks to the night after his test shift, the ground moves during his shift, at the door. Three minutes of it. The lights go, the back bar comes down, and every car alarm in the lot goes off. Then the quiet: no power, no phones, no network, no card machine, no ferry, no word from the mainland. The empire doesn't fall. It stops reaching the island.

What it does:

- **The ground.** The coast drops. The Sov, built on the old cannery's pilings, shifts and leans. Hours later the sea comes up under the floor, higher than any king tide.
- **Time.** Before, and after. Once the phones die, the time is the bar clock, which runs on a battery and was always set fast; then the tide and the light; and when the power comes back, islanded, the clocks run slow (see Island Time). At close the till can't print without power, so the last reckoning is in his hand, by candle, on the back of the incident log.
- **The town.** Cash means what people agree it means. The Sov is the one lit building on the highway (candles, and the Gold Man's generator if the Gold Man is a friend). The door becomes the town's first border.
- **The people** (DECIDED, Hutch's favourite: "the young lose the world they lived in and the old get back the one they remember, but both are experiencing something new"). The young lose a world that was in their phones. The old get back one they remember, without power or news, but they're old in it now and most of the people they remember are dead, so it's new to them too. Table 28 know how to live without a network, and the kids don't. The voices of the old, which couldn't read the young (`02_SYSTEMS.md`), are suddenly the ones that know what to do, and the young are the ones who can carry things up the hill.

### Was it helped?

The game gives two readings, all the way through, and never chooses.

**The plain one.** The quake was due, and the ground said so. Under the island the deep part of the fault slips slowly every fourteen months or so, for a couple of weeks, with a tremor nobody feels. In week three the paper reports that it's started (day 16), and the TV says the odds of the Big One are up for those two weeks, though still remote. Seismologists still argue about whether the odds really go up ([Willamette Week, on the debate](https://www.wweek.com/culture/2017/02/24/theres-a-very-slow-earthquake-under-the-puget-sound-does-this-make-the-big-one-more-likely)). The keg room pipe gives that week. The quake comes inside the window.

**The strange one.** On day 22, Wednesday 25 November, just after dark, a spiral of light opens over the strait, turns, and fades. Everyone in the lot sees it, and the Esso kid films it from behind the till. The next day it's explained, truly: a rocket's upper stage venting the last of its fuel, the same thing people have seen over Hawaii, Alaska and New Zealand, and over Britain and Europe in March 2025 ([Space.com](https://www.space.com/spacex-gps-rocket-launch-spiral-hawaii)). For a week the bar argues about it in fragments: HAARP, the cloud seeders, the psyops. Seven days later the ground moves. With Gut Brain high, he remembers that the spiral turned the same way the knot is tied.

"There is supposed to be an earthquake, but perhaps something forced natures hand." It was due. Something may have forced its hand. Both are true in the game, and the player decides how much the second one matters.

**The last night turns the job inside out** (DECIDED). For four weeks his work is keeping people out and knowing who's in. Once the ground has moved, the right thing is probably to get everyone out of an old wooden building on pilings and up the hill before the water comes. Nobody's sure, and that's the decision. Clearing the room is the closing skill he's practised every night (`02_SYSTEMS.md`), and now it saves lives. Who will go for him depends on the month: who trusts him, who owes him, whom he's 86'd, whom he knows by face, now there are no licences left to check. And table 28 won't leave their table. "We were here first." He can't make them, unless they'd follow him anywhere. Either way the table is there in the morning (DECIDED: it sustains itself to the end).

**The rats.** With terms, the old man tells him a week before that they'll be going, and on Tuesday night, day 28, the keg room is empty and the wall behind table 28 is quiet. If he listened, he knows, and since the flood on day 17 he has a joke to remember. At war, there's no warning. Neglected, they all go at once on Wednesday afternoon, out every door in daylight, a river of rats across the highway, and the whole town sees it and laughs, or doesn't.

**Day 30, the morning after:** the town, the sea, who's alive. The walk-in thaws and the meat feeds whoever made it up the hill. The reckoning reads the whole month (PROPOSED: the spike, with every night's tape on it), and the teller is revealed (PROPOSED: who's telling it depends on the playthrough).

**The public date.** Everyone counts down to something else (PROPOSED: the public date and the private event aren't the same thing). The mill's last shift: the smokers on the deck say Christmas (`deck.ink`); the notice is in Thursday's paper on day 9: Friday 27 November, day 24. The wake that night is the biggest night of the month at the door, with a strange drug going round and a brawl in the lot that he pulls apart one group at a time, a young man glassed, thirty-one stitches, and seven police SUVs half an hour late (Hutch's story 2). Everybody thinks it's the end of something. It ended on a Wednesday.

**The tells,** all month, on every layer:

- *The ground:* glasses ringing on the back bar when there are no trucks; the deck door that won't latch because the frame's moved; the pool table's lean getting worse; the water in the keg room a little higher every week, until the pipe; a swarm of tiny tremors under the island in week two, on the TV news, which the seismologists say is normal (it is: under the real island a swarm like it comes [about every fourteen months](https://gsa.confex.com/gsa/2007CD/webprogram/Paper121326.html)); the slow slip in the paper; king tides; birds off the strait; dogs.
- *The sky:* the spiral on day 22; the arguments after it; the Lightkeeper's log; clouds the bar says were seeded.
- *The island:* the card machine (built, on day 1); the truck that didn't come off the ferry; the island running short (Hutch's notes, built: the diesel, limes in eighths, NO MORE TIL TUES over a tap); sailings cancelled; the mainland's news getting shorter.
- *Time:* the bar clock against the phones, and the Esso's clock against both.
- *The strange:* dreams the voices share at night; the old man's stories of 1946 and of 1700; the rats leaving the keg room; and each eccentric seeing one face of it.
- *The teller:* "That was the first time the glasses rang. Nobody counted it."

**What he notices is up to him,** and so is what he doesn't. Gut Brain decides how many tells he sees and how much the voices make of them, and drink raises it. The Lightkeeper and the Faller teach him the ground, the Esso kid the clocks and the sky, the old man the rest. A doorman with low Gut Brain who never asks gets the end as a surprise. Both are honest.

## Replaying it

Hutch, 14 October: replayability "is something we haven't talked about enough yet, though the games systems themselves lend themselves to multiple playthroughs."

- **What's the same every game:** the town's streets and places, the cast, the calendar and its set pieces, and the Big One on day 29.
- **What changes:** the four hundred people and their lives; which fifteen or so of the forty small stories he meets (DECIDED); who he is (what he said in Gary's office, his stats, his voices, what he wears, how he drinks, how he fights); the rats' course; the reading, plain or strange; who's alive on day 30, and who tells it.
- **What the player knows and he doesn't.** On a second game the player knows the ground will move. He doesn't, and nobody in town will believe him if he says so, unless they already trust him. Knowing the end is worth exactly as much as the trust he's built, which is the game's argument in one rule.
- **The teller notices.** On a second game his first line is different: "I've told you this one before. Not like this."

## The people's look

Hutch likes his hand, which looks like wood grain. Honestly, it's half accident. The press cuts people in strokes that wrap round their form, the way a lino cutter follows a figure (`godot/look/surface.gdshader`, the cut for people). His hands are the nearest thing to his eyes, so the strokes are at their biggest there, and they bend round the knuckles and the back of the hand like growth rings round a knot. Nobody planned for it to read as carved wood.
